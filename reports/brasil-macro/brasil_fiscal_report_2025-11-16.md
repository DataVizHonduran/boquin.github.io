# Brasil Fiscal & Political Report
**Coverage Period:** October 17 – November 16, 2025
**Report Date:** November 16, 2025

## Executive Summary

Brazil's fiscal and political environment in the past 30 days has been marked by significant coalition tensions and a major cabinet reshuffle ahead of the 2026 election cycle. President Lula appointed leftist ally Guilherme Boulos to the General Secretariat on October 20, signaling a strategic pivot toward social movements amid declining coalition loyalty—now at 30-year lows. Rating agency Moody's downgraded Brazil's outlook from positive to stable in May, effectively postponing investment-grade prospects beyond the current administration. Congress concluded voting on the government's fiscal adjustment package in late November/December, projecting R$34 billion in savings for 2025, though significantly below the originally proposed R$70 billion over two years. The minimum wage reform limiting real gains to 0.6-2.5% annually is projected to save R$110 billion through 2030, with R$2 billion in 2025 alone.

## Fiscal Developments

**1. Spending Cut Package Advanced in Congress**
Congress concluded voting on the government's fiscal adjustment package in late November/December 2024, promulgating Constitutional Amendment 135 on December 20. The package comprises three legislative measures: PEC 54/2024 (now Constitutional Amendment 135), PLP 210/2024 imposing spending limits in case of deficit, and PL 4,614/2024 restricting access to the Continuous Cash Benefit (BPC) and capping real minimum wage increases. The government initially projected savings of R$70 billion over 2025-2026, but the approved package is expected to reduce expenses by approximately R$34 billion in 2025 according to Finance Minister Fernando Haddad. Key measures include changes to wage bonuses (abono salarial), limits on minimum wage growth, a prohibition on granting or extending tax incentives in case of primary deficit from 2025 onward, and annual personnel expense growth capped at 0.6% through 2030.
*Sources: Agência Brasil, Portal da Câmara dos Deputados, Senado Notícias (December 2024)*

**2. Minimum Wage Reform with Fiscal Cap**
The government implemented new rules limiting real minimum wage gains (above inflation) to a range of 0.6% to 2.5% annually, even when GDP growth exceeds those levels. The 2025 minimum wage was set at R$1,518 (7.5% increase, 2.61% real gain), while the 2026 projection is R$1,631 (7.44% increase). This fiscal framework change is estimated to generate savings of up to R$110 billion by 2030, with R$2 billion saved in 2025 alone. The adjustment formula considers accumulated INPC inflation through November of the prior year plus capped real GDP growth from two years earlier. The minimum wage serves as reference for 59.9 million Brazilians, including formal workers, retirees, and social benefit recipients, making this a significant fiscal lever.
*Sources: Dieese technical note (January 2025), PLOA 2026, Creditas Exponencial*

**3. BPC (Continuous Cash Benefit) Reforms**
In October 2025, the Ministry of Development and Social Assistance and INSS published a joint ordinance updating BPC norms to allow benefit continuation even with family income variations, provided the last month's income or 12-month average remains at or below 1/4 of the minimum wage. The reform introduces automatic conversion of BPC to auxílio-inclusão when beneficiaries with disabilities enter the labor market earning up to two minimum wages, streamlining employment incentives without requiring new applications. These changes are part of PL 4,614/2024's broader fiscal adjustment, which restricts BPC access to help contain mandatory spending growth. Additionally, the government announced it will block online betting deposits by BPC beneficiaries, following a Supreme Court ruling.
*Sources: Agência Brasil (October 2025), Ministry of Development and Social Assistance, INSS*

**4. Fiscal Target and Debt Outlook**
Brazil's official fiscal target for 2025 remains a zero primary balance (balanced budget) with a tolerance margin of 0.25% of GDP. However, IMF staff projects a modest widening in the federal primary deficit to 0.6% of GDP in 2025, up from 0.4% in 2024, indicating the government may miss its target. Central Bank data shows gross debt at 78.1% of GDP as of September 2025, with Treasury projections pointing to 84.2% by 2028. Goldman Sachs analysis suggests that stabilizing public debt would require a primary surplus above 2.5% of GDP—implying a fiscal adjustment of roughly 3 percentage points beyond the current zero deficit goal, highlighting the structural gap between announced measures and debt sustainability requirements.
*Sources: IMF Article IV Consultation (2025), Central Bank of Brazil, Goldman Sachs research (November 2025)*

**5. MP 1.303/2025 Fails; Revenue Gap Emerges**
Provisional Measure nº 1.303/2025, which proposed raising the IOF (Tax on Financial Operations) on financial applications, electronic invoices, fintechs, and control of tax credits, lost validity on October 8 after Congress voted 383-98 to withdraw it from the agenda. This marked the first time since 1992 that Congress overturned a presidential decree. The Executive branch had anticipated R$10.6 billion in revenue from MP 1.303 in 2025 and R$21 billion in 2026. The measure's failure created a significant fiscal gap requiring the Finance Ministry to identify alternative revenues within weeks before the next bimonthly fiscal report. Even parties with ministers in Lula's cabinet—including União Brasil, MDB, PSD, Republicanos, PP, PDT, and PSB—voted against the government, with only 98 deputies supporting the measure.
*Sources: Portal da Câmara dos Deputados (October 2025), Warren Investimentos*

## Political Developments

**1. Cabinet Reshuffle: Guilherme Boulos Appointed to General Secretariat**
On October 20, President Lula appointed federal lawmaker and homeless workers movement leader Guilherme Boulos as Minister of the General Secretariat of the Presidency, replacing Márcio Macêdo. The General Secretariat coordinates government outreach to social movements, a central pillar of Lula's political strategy. Boulos, who was elected federal lawmaker in 2022 and lost the 2024 São Paulo mayoral runoff, used his first address to push for greater taxation on the ultra-wealthy and betting companies as tools to boost public funds and reduce inequality. Political analysts interpret the appointment as evidence of Lula's strategic realignment toward the left ahead of the 2026 election, strengthening ties with social movements as coalition loyalty in Congress deteriorates.
*Sources: Bloomberg, U.S. News, Brasil de Fato, MercoPress (October 2025)*

**2. Coalition Loyalty Collapses to 30-Year Low**
Coalition loyalty in Congress has fallen to the lowest rate since 1995, matching levels seen during President Dilma Rousseff's 2016 impeachment crisis. In June 2025, Congress overturned Lula's decree to raise the IOF tax with 383 votes against and only 98 in favor—the first such override since 1992. Even allied parties with cabinet positions (União Brasil, MDB, PSD, Republicanos, PP, PDT, PSB) predominantly voted against the government. Lula's approval rating stands at 28% with 40% disapproval according to Datafolha, among the lowest of his three terms. As Lula's political capital weakens heading into the 2026 election year, parties see diminished incentive to support the government. Political analyst Paulo Niccoli Ramirez notes that "the more Congress acts against Lula, the stronger he becomes in public opinion," suggesting the administration may leverage opposition to mobilize its base.
*Sources: Datafolha poll, Brasil de Fato (October 2025), various political analysis*

**3. Lula Announces 2026 Reelection Bid**
On October 23, 2025, President Lula formally announced he will run for reelection in the 2026 Brazilian general election. This announcement comes amid deteriorating fiscal credibility, record-low coalition support, and intensifying tensions with the conservative-majority Congress. The reelection bid frames the political context for all subsequent fiscal negotiations, as legislators weigh electoral calculations against policy cooperation. Tensions between the Executive and Congress, already evident at the end of 2025, are expected to intensify throughout 2026, potentially constraining the government's ability to advance additional fiscal adjustments or structural reforms until after the electoral cycle concludes.
*Sources: Multiple news sources (October 2025)*

## Market & External Context

**1. Rating Agency Actions Signal Investment Grade Postponed**
In May 2025, Moody's Ratings revised Brazil's sovereign debt outlook from positive to stable, effectively ruling out investment-grade status before the end of Lula's current term. Moody's maintains Brazil at Ba1 with stable outlook. Fitch affirmed Brazil's BB rating with stable outlook in June 2025, citing the 2026 presidential elections as a factor delaying resolution of fiscal challenges until 2027. S&P also maintained its BB rating with stable outlook in June 2025. All three major agencies now project no near-term upgrade, a setback from the positive momentum of 2023-2024 when both Fitch and S&P raised Brazil to two notches below investment grade. The outlook downgrades reflect market concerns over fiscal framework credibility and political gridlock constraining adjustment capacity.
*Sources: Moody's Ratings (May 2025), Fitch Ratings (June 2025), S&P Global (June 2025)*

**2. Currency Depreciation and Fiscal Credibility Crisis**
Following the announcement of the fiscal adjustment package in late November 2024, the Brazilian real reached a record devaluation against the U.S. dollar as market participants perceived the spending cuts as lacking ambition and including future spending through income tax exemptions. The currency weakness reflects investor skepticism about Brazil's fiscal trajectory, exacerbated by the June 2025 defeat of the IOF decree and declining coalition support. By the second quarter of 2024, the fiscal strategy had reached a political limit, generating a credibility crisis that persisted into late 2025. Goldman Sachs warned in November 2025 that Brazil's fiscal discipline will be crucial post-2026 election, with fiscal reality likely to dominate the agenda regardless of electoral outcomes.
*Sources: Bloomberg, Goldman Sachs research (November 2025), Global Policy Watch*
