## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **Exceptionally low debt burden**: General government gross debt at **~32% of GDP** (2025E), among the lowest in Latin America and well below the 60% DSA benchmark; Peru's fiscal rule targets further reduction to a 30% of GDP ceiling by 2035
- **Reserve adequacy is the region's strongest**: Gross international reserves of **USD 95 billion** (January 2026), equivalent to **~15.5 months of imports** and **~26.5% of GDP** — a capital buffer that absorbs commodity cycles, capital flow reversals, and political shocks with exceptional headroom
- **External sector in structural surplus**: Current account in surplus (~**+1.7% of GDP** in 2025E) supported by copper and gold export revenues; Peru is the world's second-largest copper producer and a key beneficiary of the global energy transition metals cycle
- **Inflation within target and BCRP credibility intact**: CPI at **1.7% y/y** (January 2026), within the BCRP's 1–3% target band; monetary credibility is a key relative strength enabling accommodative policy without credibility risk; BCRP held the policy rate at 4.25% in February 2026

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Structural political instability — the core rating constraint**: Eight presidents in a decade (seven removals, resignations, or impeachments since 2016); President José María Balcázar is the 8th executive since 2016, holding a caretaker role ahead of **April 12, 2026** general elections with 34 registered candidates; institutional continuity risk is among the highest in the investment-grade universe
- **Fiscal consolidation lagging targets**: General government overall deficit of **~3.5% of GDP** (2024A) and estimated **~2.4–2.6% of GDP** (2025E) — above the fiscal rule's convergence path to 1% by 2028; primary deficit of approximately **~0.8% of GDP** (2025E) represents incremental improvement but remains below the DSA threshold
- **Mining investment pipeline stalled**: IMF Article IV (June 2025) highlighted a **USD 62 billion** mining project backlog blocked by community conflicts, permitting delays, and political uncertainty; realization of this pipeline is both the key upside growth catalyst and the key downside risk if further delayed
- **Growth below potential**: Real GDP growth of ~3.0% (2025E) and ~2.7% (2026F) trails the estimated potential rate of ~4%; political instability directly suppresses private investment sentiment and FDI inflows, creating a structural growth discount

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Peru's **gross public debt/GDP at ~32%** operates well inside the DSA danger zone, and the trajectory is mechanically favorable: nominal GDP growth (real ~3% + inflation ~2%) combined with primary balance convergence toward zero drives gradual debt reduction. The **r–g dynamic** is modestly adverse — Peru's external USD bond yields of ~6–7% against real growth of ~2.7% create a slight positive interest-growth differential (~3–4 percentage points) requiring a primary surplus of ~1% of GDP to fully stabilize the debt ratio; the current primary deficit of ~0.8% of GDP is thus marginally debt-increasing but at a pace fully absorbed by the nominal GDP deflator. The IMF fiscal rule — targeting a 1% overall deficit by 2028 — provides the institutional anchor; compliance is critical to prevent trend deterioration, but even slippage would only marginally move a 32% debt ratio over the medium term.

### 3C. External Liquidity

Gross reserves of **USD 95 billion** — equivalent to 15.5 months of imports and 26.5% of GDP — represent the most robust external liquidity buffer in Latin America, providing multi-year insulation against copper price volatility, capital flow reversals, and political uncertainty. Peru's **dollarization legacy** (approximately 25–30% of domestic banking sector deposits in USD) creates a structural demand for reserve maintenance, but the BCRP's reserve requirement regime and consistent reserve accumulation over two decades have established strong credibility; short-term external debt obligations are modest relative to the reserve stock, and rollover risk is negligible under the base case.

## 4. WILLINGNESS TO PAY

| Dimension | Score (1–5) | Commentary |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Governance & Rule of Law (WGI) | **2** | Peru ranks in the ~30th percentile for Rule of Law and ~40th percentile for Government Effectiveness (WGI 2023) — declining trend; eight presidents in a decade reflects deep institutional fragility; congressional veto over executive actions creates chronic policy paralysis |
| Fiscal Policy Credibility | **3** | The fiscal rule framework is credible in structure, but execution — a 3.5% deficit in 2024 against a 2.2% target — shows slippage; Moody's and Fitch maintain investment-grade ratings partly in expectation of post-election fiscal normalization; the very low debt level reduces urgency |
| Central Bank Independence | **5** | BCRP is widely regarded as one of Latin America's most credible and independent central banks; inflation at 1.7% (Jan 2026) is within the 1–3% target; reserve accumulation track record is exceptional; monetary credibility is the single strongest institutional pillar of Peru's credit profile |
| Debt Payment Track Record | **4** | Peru has not defaulted since the Brady Bond restructuring of 1997 — a nearly 30-year clean track record; consistent market access, active debt management, and sovereign liability management exercises demonstrate strong willingness-to-pay culture; political turmoil has not translated into debt service disruption |

**Willingness-to-Pay Synthesis:** Peru presents a bifurcated willingness-to-pay profile: outstanding debt service track record and BCRP credibility anchor solid scores, offset by governance fragility that consistently scores in the bottom tercile of investment-grade sovereigns. The political cycle — 34 candidates, April 12 elections, polling led by Lima mayor Rafael López Aliaga — creates genuine uncertainty about the post-election policy agenda (labor reform, mining permitting, fiscal rule compliance) rather than about debt payment per se. The existential risk is not an imminent default but a gradual erosion of the institutional guardrails (fiscal rule abandonment, BCRP independence encroachment) that underpin the Baa rating, particularly if a populist administration wins the presidency.

## 7. ESG CONSIDERATIONS

| ESG Factor | Assessment |
|------------|------------|
| **E – Environmental** | Peru is the world's second-largest copper and sixth-largest gold producer, making fiscal revenues highly sensitive to commodity price cycles and subject to increasing ESG scrutiny on mining environmental practices. Climate vulnerability is elevated — La Niña–driven flooding and El Niño droughts periodically disrupt agricultural production and infrastructure. The **USD 62bn stalled mining pipeline** (IMF Article IV, June 2025) partially reflects environmental permitting delays and indigenous community conflicts over land rights in the Andean highlands. **Credit-relevant:** Mining concentration creates procyclical fiscal vulnerability; permitting gridlock directly suppresses the primary credit upside scenario; ESG disputes over copper and lithium extraction could intensify under anti-mining administrations. |
| **S – Social** | Poverty rate of ~27% (2024) has declined structurally over two decades but remains elevated in rural highlands. Income inequality (Gini ~43) and regional disparities between Lima and the highlands drive recurring social conflicts over mining projects. Political volatility — including the 2022 self-coup attempt, three impeachments, two resignations, and eight governments since 2016 — reflects deep social discontent with Lima-based elites that persists across administrations. **Credit-relevant:** Social conflict over mining permits is the primary mechanism through which political instability translates into growth and fiscal constraint; any administration's inability to manage community relations in the Andes directly suppresses the USD 62bn pipeline. |
| **G – Governance** | WGI 2023: Rule of Law ~30th percentile; Government Effectiveness ~40th percentile; Control of Corruption ~35th percentile — all in the bottom half of investment-grade sovereigns. Peru has had 8 presidents since 2016, including one self-coup attempt, three impeachments, two resignations, and one presidential suicide; congressional supremacy without legislative discipline creates a hostile environment for multi-year reform programs. The BCRP stands as the single institutional bright spot with governance quality befitting an A-rated central bank. **Credit-relevant:** Governance weakness is the binding constraint on the rating; without sustained institutional improvement, macro strengths are structurally discounted and the Baa1 upgrade path remains blocked. |

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Post-election government **abandons fiscal rule** or expands the overall deficit beyond 3% of GDP for two consecutive years, triggering debt trajectory deterioration toward 40% of GDP
- **Social conflict escalates** to the point of halting major copper and gold mines (Las Bambas, Antamina, Cerro Verde), compressing export revenues and fiscal receipts by more than 1% of GDP on a sustained basis
- BCRP independence is **legislatively encroached** — change of leadership via congressional interference, monetary financing requirements — the single most acute systemic risk to Peru's credit profile and the trigger for multi-notch downgrade
- Net foreign reserves fall below **12 months of imports** on a sustained basis due to capital flight or sustained copper prices below USD 3.50/lb

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Post-election government with a stable congressional coalition implements **structural mining reform** that unlocks the USD 62bn pipeline, lifting GDP growth durably to 5%+ and fiscal revenues by 1–2% of GDP over the forecast horizon
- **Primary fiscal surplus** achieved ahead of the 2028 fiscal rule schedule, demonstrating institutional compliance through a full electoral cycle — the most credible signal of governance improvement
- Rule of Law WGI percentile improves from ~30th to **above 45th percentile** over two consecutive surveys, signaling durable institutional normalization
- Copper prices sustained above **USD 5.00/lb** for 2+ years, significantly widening the current account surplus and creating fiscal space materially beyond the base case

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Peru is the purest example of an "institutional discount" credit in the investment-grade EM universe: its macro balance sheet — ~32% debt/GDP, 15.5 months of import cover, 1.7% inflation, a current account surplus, and near-perfect BCRP credibility — would comfortably support a Baa1 or even A3 rating on quantitative metrics alone. The discount imposed by political governance risk — 8 presidents in a decade, April 2026 elections with 34 candidates, and chronic congressional-executive deadlock — is both real and structurally embedded; until Peru demonstrates that political transitions translate into policy continuity rather than policy reversal, the spread premium over the Philippines and Uruguay will persist. The April 12, 2026 election is the single most important near-term credit event: a reform-friendly outcome with a workable congressional coalition could unlock the USD 62bn mining pipeline and justify upgrade momentum toward Baa1; a populist outcome with anti-mining or anti-fiscal rule positioning would shift the outlook to Negative and pressure the current rating floor. In the base case, macro resilience absorbs political volatility without rating dislocation — a pattern Peru has demonstrated across multiple administrations.

> **We assign Peru an implied rating of Baa2 with a Stable outlook, reflecting exceptional macro fundamentals and BCRP credibility offset by structural political instability that constrains governance scores and suppresses the mining investment pathway critical to long-term growth.**
