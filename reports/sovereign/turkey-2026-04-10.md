## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **Ultra-low sovereign debt with primary balance now positive**: General government debt at **~25.3% of GDP** (Q1 2025) remains among the lowest in the EM universe, providing extraordinary DSA headroom. Critically, the primary balance turned positive in January–February 2026 — the primary surplus reached **TRY 241.9bn in January** and **TRY 208.1bn in February 2026**, reversing deficits from a year earlier — signaling structural fiscal consolidation beyond the earthquake reconstruction distortions of 2024.
- **IMF 2025 Article IV endorses disinflation path**: The IMF Executive Board concluded the 2025 Article IV Consultation on **February 13, 2026** (Staff Country Report No. 26/43), explicitly noting that Turkey's disinflation program "has shown successes" — inflation fell from 49.4% (September 2024) to 30.9% (December 2025). The IMF recommends continued fiscal tightening but does not flag systemic risks, providing an important institutional anchor for investor confidence.
- **Growth resilient**: Real GDP grew **4.1% in 2025** (IMF Article IV); IMF WEO January 2026 projects **4.2% for 2026**, supported by recovering real wages, easing credit conditions, and investment recovery. World Bank projects **3.7%** for 2026. Either figure is above the 3% DSA favorable benchmark.
- **Fitch Positive Outlook signals rating convergence**: Fitch's January 2026 upgrade of Turkey's outlook to Positive (BB–/Positive) — citing FX reserve recovery and reduced policy reversal risk — reduces the agency divergence from Moody's and increases the probability of a coordinated upgrade cycle to Ba2/BB territory within 12–18 months, contingent on sustained disinflation.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Current account deficit widened materially**: Turkey's current account deficit widened from **$10.42bn in 2024** to **$25.21bn in 2025** — a 142% deterioration. January 2026 recorded a monthly deficit of $6.8bn, suggesting the trend is not yet reversing. While the deficit as a % of GDP (~2.3%) remains within the IMF's -3% benchmark, the speed and scale of widening represents a material credit deterioration from the prior report's estimate of -0.8% to -1.5% of GDP.
- **Inflation plateaued; TCMB easing paused**: After falling from 49.4% (September 2024) to 30.9% (December 2025), inflation edged up to **31.53%** (February 2026) before partially recovering to **30.87%** (March 2026). The TCMB cut rates from 38% → 37% in January 2026 (less than the market's 1.5pp consensus) and then **held at 37% in March 2026** — the first hold after five consecutive cuts — citing regional conflict and energy price pressures. The IMF's end-2026 inflation forecast of **23%** requires a significant acceleration in disinflation that is not currently visible in the monthly data.
- **Governance and political risk elevated**: Turkey ranked **117th out of 142 countries** in the World Justice Project Rule of Law Index (2024) and received a Freedom House score of **33/100 ("Not Free")**. Balkan Insight's January 2026 analysis characterizes Turkey as facing "turbulence at home, uncertainty abroad." Mass protests, Erdoğan's atrophied institutional capacity (loyalty-based appointments replacing meritocracy), and AKP internal succession maneuvering ahead of the 2028 elections create event risk that is not priced into current spreads.
- **Gross external financing requirement is very high**: Turkey's external debt reached **$564.9bn** (38% of GDP, Q3 2025). Gross external financing needs — combining current account deficit financing and short-term debt rollover — are estimated at **~$225bn annually (30% of GDP)**. Even with gross reserves of ~$145–160bn (including gold and swaps), the external refinancing burden creates persistent vulnerability to sudden stop risk and requires continuous market access.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Turkey's debt/GDP trajectory is the least concerning DSA metric in this sovereign note — at ~25% of GDP and with nominal GDP growth (real ~4% + inflation ~31% = ~35% nominal growth), the debt denominator expands rapidly, keeping the ratio stable or slightly improving despite the ~3.5–3.7% overall fiscal deficit. The IMF baseline projects debt/GDP to remain in the 25–28% range through 2030, providing a substantial buffer against any fiscal shock. The relevant fiscal risk is not sustainability per se but credibility: the persistence of an overall deficit above 3% of GDP while inflation remains at 31% sends a mixed signal on the disinflation commitment, and the IMF's Article IV explicitly recommended bringing the deficit "temporarily below the 3% medium-term target" to support disinflation. The primary balance improvement (turning positive in January–February 2026) is the most significant positive fiscal signal in this update cycle.

### 3C. External Liquidity

Turkey's external liquidity picture is more complex than the headline figures suggest. FX-only reserves of $65.7bn (February 2026, down from $76.6bn in January) represent only **2.2 months of import cover** — below the IMF's 3-month benchmark — though total gross reserves including gold and central bank swap lines are estimated at $145–160bn, which is a more adequate buffer. The critical concern is Turkey's gross external financing requirement of ~$225bn annually (30% of GDP): even with $145bn+ in gross reserves and the rebuilt reserve cushion since 2023, any deterioration in market confidence — including a risk-off episode triggered by regional conflict — could stress rollover capacity and require the TCMB to maintain elevated policy rates in defense of the TRY, in direct tension with the disinflation objective.

**Willingness-to-Pay Synthesis:** Turkey's willingness to pay is structurally supported by a debt service track record that has withstood severe shocks, and the Şimşek-Karahan framework is the strongest institutional anchor in the Turkish policy apparatus since the 2002–2007 reform era. The TCMB's decision to cut rates by less than expected in January 2026 — when political pressure for aggressive cuts was evident — is a marginally positive governance signal. The core constraint is that Turkey's institutional independence is leader-dependent, not system-embedded: Erdoğan's demonstrated capacity to override institutional logic (2021–2023) means willingness-to-pay assessments must always carry a political event risk premium. Mass protests (2025–2026), AKP succession dynamics, and the 2028 election cycle represent the primary political risk vectors.

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- TCMB forced to reverse rate cuts before inflation falls below **20%** (political pressure or TRY defense); inflation reaccelerates above 40% on energy shock or currency depreciation spiral
- Current account deficit widens above **−4% of GDP** (from current −2.3%), depleting FX reserves below **$50bn** and requiring emergency IMF engagement
- Political event risk materializes: Erdoğan health event, AKP succession conflict, or Şimşek departure triggers market confidence shock and reserve drawdown
- Gross external financing needs cannot be rolled over at reasonable cost; Eurobond issuance fails or is priced at spreads above 500 bps
- Geopolitical escalation (regional conflict spillover, NATO/US tensions, Greece dispute) triggers defense spending surge or sanctions risk

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Headline inflation falls durably below **20%** by Q3 2026, validating TCMB credibility and enabling controlled rate normalization toward 25–28% by year-end
- Current account deficit narrows back toward **−1% of GDP** through tourism recovery, export diversification, or energy price normalization
- S&P upgrades Turkey to **BB Stable** — all three major agencies at BB or above — triggering IG inclusion eligibility flows and spread compression toward 150 bps
- Primary surplus maintained at **>1% of GDP** for two consecutive fiscal years, demonstrating structural fiscal consolidation beyond the earthquake reconstruction base effect
- Governance improvements validated by measurable WGI percentile gain: judicial reforms, press freedom improvements, or strengthened TCMB legislative independence

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Turkey's Ba3/Positive is affirmed on the basis of a macro framework that has delivered genuine stabilization since mid-2023: IMF-endorsed disinflation progress (49.4% → 30.9% in 15 months), a primary balance that turned positive in January–February 2026, ultra-low sovereign debt at 25% of GDP, and a reserve rebuild from crisis-level negative net positions to gross reserves of $145–160bn. The Fitch Positive Outlook upgrade in January 2026 and the clean IMF Article IV (February 2026) are institutional validations of the Şimşek framework's credibility. However, this update cycle introduces three material changes from the prior February 2026 report that moderate the upgrade case: the current account deficit more than doubled to $25.21bn in 2025 (from $10.42bn in 2024), inflation has plateaued at 31% and the TCMB has paused its easing cycle amid regional conflict, and FX-only reserves have declined to 2.2 months of import cover. The path to Ba2 exists — it requires inflation declining to ~20%, the current account reverting toward balance, and the TCMB resuming rate cuts in controlled fashion — but the timeline has extended to 12–18 months rather than the near-term expectation embedded in the prior analysis.

> **We assign Turkey an implied rating of Ba3 with a Positive Outlook, reflecting genuine IMF-endorsed macro stabilization under the Şimşek framework, constrained by persistent 31% inflation, a materially widening current account deficit, and an institutional governance ceiling imposed by leader-dependent policy credibility in a hypercentralized political system.**
