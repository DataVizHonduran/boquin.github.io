## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **Exceptional primary surplus**: Egypt achieved a primary fiscal surplus of **+3.5% of GDP in FY2024/25** — the highest in the MENA region — with authorities targeting **+4.8% in FY2025/26** and **+5.0% in FY2026/27**, reflecting structural fiscal consolidation that is driving debt/GDP lower.
- **Reserve rebuild remarkable**: Net international reserves rose to **USD 51.5bn** (December 2025), representing **6.3 months of import cover** (CBE Governor, January 2026); the FX reserve accumulation follows the March 2024 managed float liberalization that unlocked $50bn in GCC bilateral support.
- **Disinflation accelerating**: Headline CPI fell to **10.1% y/y** (January 2026), from 23.2% one year earlier, enabling the CBE to cut rates 100 bps in December 2025; the policy rate at 20% still provides substantial positive real rates that anchor the disinflation path.
- **IMF program milestone reached**: 5th and 6th EFF reviews completed (December 2025), unlocking **$2.5bn**; RSF first review also cleared; total program compliance record is intact across all reviews.
- **Diversified hard-currency revenue base**: Tourism ($16.7bn, +16.3% FY2024/25), remittances (record $36.5bn, +55.3% FY2024/25), and Suez Canal fees ($10.5bn in FY2025/26, partial recovery) provide three distinct hard-currency inflow channels.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Interest burden is structurally extreme**: Interest payments consume **87% of tax revenues** and exceed 10% of GDP — among the highest ratios globally — making fiscal space extremely thin outside the primary surplus; any revenue shortfall or growth miss rapidly erodes the overall deficit position.
- **Military-dominated economy constrains structural reform**: The Egyptian military controls an estimated 25–40% of the non-oil economy through opaque conglomerates; Sisi's reluctance to privatize military-owned companies — a core IMF program requirement — creates persistent execution risk and deters the private FDI needed to lift potential growth.
- **Suez Canal structural vulnerability**: Canal revenues fell **45.5% in FY2024/25** due to Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping; any re-escalation in the Gaza conflict or regional instability would immediately reduce a revenue stream that contributes ~3% of GDP, creating procyclical fiscal pressure.
- **Gross public debt very high**: General government debt at **~86% of GDP** (June 2025), declining from 96% in 2023 but still elevated; the debt-to-tax-revenue ratio is among the highest for B-rated sovereigns, making the fiscal position highly sensitive to interest rate and growth shocks.
- **Current account deficit persistent**: CA deficit of $15.4bn (FY2024/25), narrowing but structural; sustained by remittances and tourism rather than export diversification, making it vulnerable to bilateral relationship risk (Gulf, diaspora) and geopolitical disruptions.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Egypt's gross public debt/GDP is projected to decline from ~86% (June 2025) toward the government's 90% ceiling target for June 2026, then continue lower toward 75–80% by 2030 under the IMF's baseline scenario, driven by a primary surplus of 4–5% of GDP that compensates for a structurally adverse **r–g differential**. With CBE policy rates at 20–21% and average domestic borrowing costs well above nominal GDP growth (real 4.7% + inflation ~10% = ~15% nominal), the **r–g gap on EGP debt alone is approximately 5–6 percentage points** — meaning Egypt requires a primary surplus of **4–5% of GDP just to prevent debt from self-reinforcing**. This explains why the IMF has designed the EFF program around exceptional primary surplus targets; a reversal or growth disappointment would rapidly stall or reverse debt reduction.

### 3C. External Liquidity

Reserves of $51.5bn (December 2025) representing 6.3 months of import cover comfortably exceed the IMF's minimum adequacy threshold, and the CBE notes reserves also cover approximately 158% of short-term external debt. However, the **composition of reserves matters**: an estimated $25–30bn reflects non-resident "hot money" inflows into EGP T-bills (carry trade), which are highly reversible in risk-off conditions; the underlying reserve cushion net of these volatile portfolio flows is considerably thinner than the headline figure suggests, and a sudden reversal of non-resident positions — as occurred in 2022–2023 — could rapidly compress effective liquidity.

**Willingness-to-Pay Synthesis:** Egypt's track record of debt service is strong relative to peers in this rating bucket — there have been no defaults or restructurings of external obligations, and the IMF program has been honored across all six reviews over a multi-year period. Fiscal credibility is elevated by the extraordinary primary surplus achievement (3.5–4.8% GDP), which required politically costly subsidy cuts and exchange rate adjustment. The primary constraint on willingness-to-pay scores is governance: the military's economic entrenchment creates structural reform resistance (particularly on privatization commitments), which is the central area of IMF-Egypt friction, as reflected in the delayed and combined 5th/6th reviews.

## 7. ESG CONSIDERATIONS

| ESG Factor | Assessment |

| **E – Environmental** | Egypt faces acute water scarcity risk (Nile water stress, Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam tensions) and extreme heat vulnerability, with agriculture employing ~25% of the workforce highly exposed to climate-driven yield disruptions. The Suez Canal — responsible for ~12% of global trade — creates physical climate risk exposure (water level changes affect transit capacity). **Credit-relevant:** Water scarcity creates contingent fiscal liability (agricultural support, food subsidies) and geopolitical risk with Ethiopia; GERD tensions could escalate to military conflict affecting regime stability. |

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Primary fiscal balance slips below **+2% of GDP** due to subsidy reversal, electoral pressures, or revenue shortfall — triggering debt trajectory reversal and IMF program jeopardy
- **Suez Canal revenues collapse again** (>30% decline) from renewed Red Sea conflict escalation, removing ~$3–4bn from current account receipts
- Non-resident T-bill flight (currently ~$30bn) triggers **EGP depreciation** beyond the crawling band, forcing FX intervention and reserve drawdown below 4 months of cover
- IMF program delays or fails to reach Board approval after Autumn 2026 expiry, removing the credibility anchor for a new financing framework
- Privatization pipeline stalls below **$5bn per year**, signaling military entrenchment has won over reform commitments

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Inflation declines durably below **9%** (CBE target midpoint) by Q4 2026, enabling rate cuts that reduce domestic interest burden and crowding-out of private sector
- Primary surplus maintained above **4% of GDP** for two consecutive fiscal years, placing gross debt/GDP on a **confirmed path to below 80% by 2028**
- Privatization program generates **>$10bn** in receipts in FY2026/27, demonstrating military economy rollback and unlocking FDI inflows
- Suez Canal revenues fully normalize (**>$10bn annually**) on sustained basis, restoring the pre-conflict external receipts base

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Egypt is executing one of the most ambitious fiscal consolidation programs in the emerging world: a primary surplus of 3.5% of GDP in FY2024/25, rising toward 4.8–5.0% in the next two years, combined with the disinflation of a 40%-peak inflation rate to 10.1% y/y in just two years, represents a genuine macroeconomic stabilization. The $51.5bn in reserves (6.3 months of imports), record remittance inflows ($36.5bn in FY2024/25), and intact IMF program compliance across six reviews justify S&P and Fitch's upgrade to B. The Moody's Caa1/Positive clearly anticipates convergence, and our B3 implied rating reflects that an upgrade to B2 equivalence is likely within 12 months, conditional on continued fiscal discipline. The key structural caveat — missed or underweighted by current agency ratings in our view — is that Egypt's interest-to-tax-revenue ratio of 87% leaves essentially zero fiscal cushion; the primary surplus is not a policy choice so much as an arithmetic necessity to prevent debt from spiraling. The Suez Canal vulnerability (proven by the 45.5% revenue collapse in FY2024/25) and the military's structural resistance to privatization — the core of unresolved IMF conditionality — create tail risks that keep the implied rating one notch below S&P and Fitch.

> **We assign Egypt an implied rating of B3 (Moody's equivalent) with a Positive outlook, reflecting exceptional primary surplus discipline and reserve rebuild offset by extreme interest burden, military economy entrenchment, and Suez Canal tail risk.**
