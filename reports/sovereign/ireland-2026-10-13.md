# Ireland — Sovereign Credit Note | IMF-DSA Framework | Moody's/Fitch Style | Report Date: 2026-10-13

*Note: Ireland is a developed, high-grade euro-area sovereign whose headline GDP is distorted by multinational-enterprise (MNE) activity. This note foregrounds GNI* (Modified Gross National Income), the Irish authorities' and IMF's preferred deglobalized measure, alongside headline GDP. EMBIG spread is replaced by the Irish 10Y-Bund spread; reserve-adequacy metrics are presented with the caveat that they sit within the ECB/Eurosystem framework.*

## §1 Rating Action & Outlook

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ireland | 2026-10-13 | **AA/Aa3** | Positive | Aa3 (Moody's, Positive, Aug-2025) / AA (Fitch, Stable, May-2026) / AA+ (S&P, Stable, Mar-2026) | Affirm with positive momentum — S&P's March 2026 upgrade to AA+ brings Ireland within one notch of AAA for the first time since 2009. |

Ireland's rating trajectory has been consistently upward: S&P upgraded to AA+ with a restored Stable outlook in March 2026 — within one notch of AAA for the first time since the post-crisis era — citing strong economic and budgetary performance, strengthening fiscal buffers, and continued decline in net debt [[sources below]]. Public debt fell to roughly 33% of GDP (62% of GNI*) at end-2025, and the general government posted a fiscal surplus of 1.8% of GDP (3.3% of GNI*) in 2025. The core credit tension, flagged consistently by the IMF, is that this strong headline position rests heavily on corporate income tax receipts concentrated in a small number of multinational taxpayers.

## §2 Key Rating Drivers

**Strengths**

- **Rapidly declining debt burden.** Public debt fell to ~33% of GDP (62% of GNI*) at end-2025, among the lowest in the eurozone on the GDP measure and moderate even on the more conservative GNI* basis, with net debt continuing to decline (S&P, March 2026).
- **Sustained fiscal surpluses.** Ireland posted a general government surplus of 1.8% of GDP (3.3% of GNI*) in 2025, with corporate income tax revenue outperforming budget projections on stronger-than-expected MNE profits.
- **Large current account and GNI* growth.** The current account balance is forecast at 8.1% of GDP in 2025 and 7.9% in 2026 (European Commission/CSO), while real GNI* — the deglobalized growth measure — expanded 4.7% in 2025.

**Constraints**

- **Extreme corporate tax concentration risk.** The IMF explicitly flags that the top 10 corporate taxpayers account for more than 50% of Ireland's total corporate income tax (CIT), a vulnerability that ties a large share of the fiscal surplus to the profitability and location decisions of a handful of multinationals.
- **Headline GDP distortion.** GDP volatility driven by MNE activity is severe — GDP is estimated to have contracted 17% year-over-year in Q1 2026 alone, reflecting a reversal of prior export front-loading — making headline GDP-based metrics unreliable without the GNI* adjustment.
- **Moderately expansionary fiscal stance.** The IMF's 2026 Article IV assesses the fiscal stance in 2025-26 as moderately expansionary, with the structural primary balance set to deteriorate by about 0.6% of GDP in 2026, and recommends holding to the Medium-Term Fiscal Structural Plan's expenditure ceiling to return to fiscal neutrality from 2027.

## §3B Debt Trajectory

Ireland's debt trajectory is one of the strongest in the eurozone on both headline and adjusted bases. Public debt fell to roughly 33% of GDP, or 62% of GNI*, at end-2025 — a dramatic improvement from the post-financial-crisis peak — driven by a combination of strong nominal growth and sustained fiscal surpluses, including a 1.8%-of-GDP (3.3%-of-GNI*) surplus in 2025 on corporate tax outperformance. The IMF's 2026 Article IV, however, flags that the fiscal stance is turning moderately expansionary, with the structural primary balance set to deteriorate by about 0.6% of GDP in 2026 and remain broadly unchanged thereafter; staff recommend adherence to the Medium-Term Fiscal Structural Plan's expenditure ceiling to restore a broadly neutral stance from 2027.

The structural fragility beneath this strong headline position is corporate tax concentration: the IMF is explicit that the top 10 corporate taxpayers generate more than half of Ireland's total CIT receipts, a dependency the Fund has repeatedly urged Ireland to address by broadening the tax base toward personal income, VAT, and property taxes, and by channeling "excess" corporate tax windfalls into Ireland's two sovereign savings funds rather than permanent spending commitments. This is the single most important forward-looking DSA risk factor: a shift in global minimum-tax rules, a change in a handful of MNEs' Irish footprint, or a US tax-policy shift could remove a disproportionate share of current revenue with limited warning.

## §3C External Liquidity

Ireland's external accounts are exceptionally strong on paper but require careful interpretation given MNE distortion. The current account balance is forecast at 8.1% of GDP in 2025 and 7.9% in 2026 (European Commission/CSO), while the "modified" current account (CA*, which excludes globalization effects such as redomiciled-company and contract-manufacturing flows) recorded a surplus of €24.3 billion, or 4.0% of GDP, in 2025 — still solidly positive but a fraction of the headline figure. As a euro-area member, Ireland has no independent reserve-adequacy constraint; FX backstop functions sit with the Eurosystem.

Net external debt was reported at -352% of GDP (September 2025, Eurostat) — a strongly negative figure indicating Ireland is a very large net external creditor on a headline basis, though this too is substantially inflated by MNE balance-sheet structures (intra-group financing, IP-holding vehicles) rather than reflecting genuine sovereign or household external asset accumulation. Sovereign external-liquidity risk is low by any reasonable reading of these figures; the real external vulnerability channel for Ireland runs through the stability of MNE investment and tax-residency decisions, not a classical balance-of-payments or reserves crisis.

## §8 Rating Sensitivities

**Downgrade triggers**
- Material unwinding of corporate tax concentration (e.g., top-10 taxpayer base narrows further or relocates)
- Structural primary balance deterioration continuing beyond the IMF's 2026 estimate without expenditure-ceiling adherence from 2027
- Global minimum-tax or US tax-policy shifts that durably reduce Ireland's MNE tax-residency advantage

**Upgrade triggers**
- Further tax-base broadening per IMF recommendations, reducing CIT concentration risk
- Continued net debt decline toward the lower end of eurozone peers on both GDP and GNI* bases
- Sustained fiscal surpluses channeled into savings funds, building credible buffers against future CIT volatility

## §9 Analyst Conclusion

Ireland's credit profile has strengthened consistently and materially — S&P's move to AA+ in March 2026 puts the sovereign within one notch of AAA for the first time since the crisis era, underpinned by a rapidly declining debt burden, sustained fiscal surpluses, and strong GNI*-adjusted growth. The IMF's repeated, explicit warning about corporate tax concentration — more than half of CIT receipts from just ten taxpayers — remains the defining structural vulnerability behind an otherwise exemplary set of headline metrics, and is the key risk factor that distinguishes Ireland from true AAA peers.

> **We assign Ireland an implied rating of AA/Aa3 with a Positive Outlook, reflecting a rapidly improving debt and fiscal position on both headline and GNI*-adjusted bases, set against a structurally concentrated corporate tax revenue base that remains the central, IMF-flagged vulnerability to monitor.**
