## §1 Rating Action & Outlook

| Sovereign | Date | Implied Rating | Outlook | Prior Ratings (Moody's/Fitch/S&P) | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Italy | 2026-10-13 | **BBB+/Baa2** | Positive/Stable | Baa2 (Moody's, Stable) / BBB+ (Fitch, Stable) / BBB+ (S&P, Positive) | Affirm, with positive momentum — Italy is the only large eurozone sovereign on an improving rating trajectory in 2025-2026. |

Italy has run counter to the regional trend over the past year: Moody's upgraded to Baa2 from Baa3 (2025-11-21, Stable outlook), Fitch upgraded to BBB+ from BBB (2025-09-19, Stable), and S&P raised to BBB+ (2025-04-11) before moving the outlook to Positive on 2026-01-30, citing economic resilience and current-account surpluses [[sources below]]. Debt/GDP remains the second-highest in the eurozone at roughly 138% (2026, per IMF-adjacent estimates), but a sustained primary surplus and continued NRRP-linked investment have driven multi-year spread compression even as French and (to a lesser extent) peer spreads widened — a genuine divergence story within the single currency area.

## §2 Key Rating Drivers

**Strengths**

- **Primary surplus restored and strengthening.** A better-than-expected 2024 fiscal outturn returned Italy to primary surplus, and the IMF's 2026 Article IV (concluded 2026-07-24) notes the primary surplus has continued to strengthen into 2025-26, the key precondition for a downward debt/GDP path.
- **Current account surplus.** Italy ran a current account surplus of 1.1-1.2% of GDP in 2025 (European Commission forecast), a structural external strength cited explicitly by S&P in its January 2026 outlook upgrade.
- **Political and policy stability.** Moody's upgrade rationale explicitly cited "political and policy stability and prospects of further policy actions supporting growth and fiscal consolidation" beyond the NRRP's August 2026 deadline — a marked contrast to France's fragmentation.

**Constraints**

- **Very high debt stock.** Public debt sits near 138% of GDP (2026 estimate), the second-highest in the eurozone, with interest payments consuming roughly 4.6% of GDP — a heavy fixed cost that leaves little room for fiscal error.
- **Structurally weak growth.** Real GDP growth was just 0.5% in 2025 and is projected at only 0.5-0.8% in 2026 (IMF WEO/Article IV), constrained by weak productivity and adverse demographics — the IMF flags these as persistent medium-term drags.
- **Interest-rate and growth-shock sensitivity.** The IMF's 2026 Article IV explicitly states debt "remains too high and vulnerable to interest and growth shocks," and BTP yields have risen to multi-year highs (10Y BTP above 4.62% in 2026) alongside a broader eurozone bond selloff.

## §3B Debt Trajectory

Italy's debt trajectory is the clearest counter-example within the large eurozone sovereigns of consolidation working: the 2024 fiscal outturn beat expectations and returned the primary balance to surplus, and the IMF's 2026 Article IV Concluding Statement (2026-05-27) and subsequent Executive Board conclusion (2026-07-24) both confirm the primary surplus has continued to strengthen into 2025-26. That said, the starting debt stock — roughly 138% of GDP — is high enough that even a sustained primary surplus only slowly bends the trajectory downward, and the IMF explicitly flags remaining vulnerability "to interest and growth shocks" given how large the gap between nominal growth (sub-2%) and effective borrowing costs (BTP yields >4.6%) has become.

Italy's financing needs remain elevated — the European Commission's 2025 Debt Sustainability Monitor groups Italy with Belgium, France and Finland as facing high gross financing requirements, "mostly due to maturing debt and budget deficits" — meaning rollover volume is large even if the debt mix (largely domestic-currency, euro-denominated, retail-investor-supported via BTP Italia/Più retail placements) is relatively benign on currency risk. The October 2026 BTP-Bund spread jump — the largest daily move since 2020 — is a reminder that market sentiment can move quickly even against an improving ratings backdrop, particularly amid a broader eurozone-wide bond selloff rather than an Italy-idiosyncratic shock.

## §3C External Liquidity

Italy's external position is a genuine credit strength. The current account ran a surplus of 1.1-1.2% of GDP in 2025, narrowing to a European Commission-forecast 0.5% in 2026, but remaining in surplus territory — a structural improvement from Italy's pre-2010s deficit-prone profile and a factor S&P cited explicitly in its January 2026 outlook upgrade to Positive. As a euro-area member, Italy has no independent reserve-adequacy constraint in the EM sense; Banca d'Italia held roughly €307 billion in reserves as of 2025, pooled within the Eurosystem framework rather than functioning as a standalone FX backstop.

Gross external debt stood at roughly 125% of GDP (Q1-2026, Trading Economics/CEIC), a whole-economy measure including bank and corporate cross-border liabilities, almost entirely euro-denominated. Combined with the current account surplus, Italy's external financing requirement is modest relative to its fiscal (domestic) financing need, and sudden-stop risk of the classical EM type is not a relevant channel — the transmission mechanism for stress is, as with France, spread widening on the sovereign curve rather than a balance-of-payments crisis.

## §8 Rating Sensitivities

**Downgrade triggers**
- Primary surplus reverses or fails to sustain beyond the NRRP's August 2026 completion
- BTP-Bund spread widening proves durable, raising average borrowing costs materially above nominal growth
- Growth undershoots the IMF's already-modest 0.5-0.8% 2026 baseline

**Upgrade triggers**
- Primary surplus widens further, putting debt/GDP on a clear, sustained downward path
- Growth outperforms via NRRP investment spillovers and productivity gains
- Continued political stability translates into further structural reform beyond the NRRP deadline

## §9 Analyst Conclusion

Italy stands out among the large eurozone sovereigns as the one credit story moving in a clearly positive direction: a restored and strengthening primary surplus, a current account surplus, and sustained political stability have driven three rating upgrades across agencies within the past 18 months, even as France has been downgraded over the same period. The constraint remains the sheer size of the debt stock — at ~138% of GDP, Italy has far less margin for error than peers, and the October 2026 BTP-Bund spread jump is a live reminder that market sentiment can reverse quickly within a broader eurozone bond selloff.

> **We assign Italy an implied rating of BBB+/Baa2 with a Positive Outlook, reflecting a genuinely improving fiscal trajectory — a sustained primary surplus and current account surplus — set against a still-very-high debt stock that leaves limited buffer against interest-rate or growth shocks.**
