## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **IMF EFF on track**: All quantitative performance criteria (QPCs) met for the 4th review (December 2025); $5 bn 48-month EFF arrangement (approved May 2024, augmented July 2025) provides a credibility anchor and liquidity backstop through 2028.
- **Fiscal overperformance**: NFPS overall deficit narrowed to ~0.7% of GDP in 2025, outperforming the IMF consolidation path; primary surplus estimated at ~+2.5% of GDP provides positive debt dynamics in the medium term.
- **Debt on firm downward trajectory**: Public debt/GDP declining from ~55% (2023) toward a COPLAFIP statutory target of 45% by 2030, with meaningful deleveraging space confirmed by IMF's 4th review.
- **Market re-entry successful**: Ecuador placed $4 billion in 8- and 13-year bonds on January 26, 2026 (priced at 8.75% and 9.25%), its first major international market issuance since restructuring in 2020 — a decisive confidence signal.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Security crisis structurally unresolved**: Ecuador carries the highest homicide rate in Latin America (+40% YoY through mid-2025); organized crime penetration raises contingent fiscal liability risk (security spending) and depresses FDI and tourism inflows.
- **Dollarization removes monetary flexibility**: With the U.S. dollar as legal tender, Ecuador has no independent monetary policy; all external shocks must be absorbed through fiscal adjustment or wage/price deflation, amplifying vulnerability to external funding shortfalls.
- **Thin reserve buffer**: Despite improvement to ~3.3 months of imports (mid-2025), reserves remain at the lower bound of adequacy; a shock to oil revenues or external financing would quickly erode this cushion.
- **Political weakening constrains reform pace**: Constitutional referendum rejected by voters (November 2025); Noboa administration enters 2026 local election season weakened, reducing legislative bandwidth for structural reforms needed to durably improve the growth trajectory.
- **History of serial defaults**: Ecuador defaulted in 2008 and 2020; willingness-to-pay assessments remain structurally discounted despite current compliance, as institutional memory of restructuring remains fresh among creditors.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Ecuador's public debt/GDP is projected to decline from ~51% (2025E) toward 45% by 2030, consistent with COPLAFIP statutory targets, underpinned by a primary surplus of approximately 2.5% of GDP. The critical dynamic is the **r–g differential**: with new bond pricing at 8.75–9.25% and nominal GDP growth of approximately 4.6% (2.2% real + 2.4% inflation), the implicit interest rate on the public debt stock exceeds the growth rate by roughly 3–4 percentage points. This means Ecuador **requires a sustained primary surplus of at least 1.5–2% of GDP to prevent debt from rebounding** — a threshold currently met but dependent on continued IMF program compliance and oil production stability. Downside scenarios (commodity price collapse, security-related FDI contraction) could rapidly flip the trajectory.

### 3C. External Liquidity

Ecuador's dollarized framework means international reserves serve as the sole liquidity buffer, with no ability to print currency in a crisis. At 3.3 months of imports (mid-2025), reserves are barely at the IMF's minimum adequacy threshold; IMF obligations peaking at ~15% of gross reserves in 2025 add further pressure on the net reserve position, and any deterioration in oil export revenues or tourism receipts could push reserve cover back below 3 months within 6–12 months.

However, Ecuador's serial default history (2008, 2020), combined with the absence of independent monetary policy and a security crisis that strains fiscal resources, creates a structurally lower baseline for willingness-to-pay scores than headline fiscal metrics suggest. A leadership change or external shock that disrupts IMF program continuity would likely re-trigger market stress given the absence of a monetary buffer.

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Public debt/GDP rises above **60%** on a sustained basis, signaling primary balance deterioration or debt-financed security spending
- IMF EFF program suspended or materially off-track due to missed QPCs (e.g., primary balance slippage >1% GDP) or political inability to pass required legislation
- Foreign exchange reserves fall below **2.0 months of imports**, triggering liquidity stress in the dollarized framework
- Security crisis escalates materially, forcing FDI exit and depressing non-oil GDP growth below **1%** for 2+ consecutive years
- Voluntary or involuntary debt restructuring initiated (third sovereign default in 20 years)

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Primary surplus sustained above **2% of GDP** for 2+ consecutive years, placing debt/GDP firmly below **45%** by 2028
- Successful IMF EFF completion (2028) followed by voluntary exit without market stress, confirming durable market access
- Homicide rate declines materially (>30% reduction sustained 2+ years), reducing security spending drag and unlocking FDI into tourism, agriculture, and manufacturing
- Constitutional framework stabilized (new constitution or durable political accord), enabling structural reforms to attract non-oil FDI and diversify export base

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Ecuador presents a compelling but fragile recovery story. The Noboa administration has delivered meaningful fiscal outperformance — the NFPS deficit narrowed to ~0.7% of GDP in 2025, well inside IMF targets, while all four EFF review QPCs were met — and the January 2026 $4 billion bond issuance marked a decisive return to international capital markets, with spreads compressing from ~1,900 bps (April 2025) to ~430 bps. These achievements justify Fitch's upgrade to B– and Moody's two-notch move to Caa1. However, Ecuador's structural vulnerabilities are not resolved: the dollarized framework leaves the sovereign without a lender-of-last-resort function, reserve coverage at 3.3 months of imports sits at minimum adequacy, the security crisis remains uncontained, and the constitutional referendum defeat reduces reform execution capacity heading into 2026 local elections. The r–g differential (implicit cost of debt ~9% vs. nominal GDP growth ~4.6%) requires a sustained primary surplus of 2%+ of GDP — achievable under current conditions but brittle under shock scenarios. Convergence to B3 (Moody's equivalent to Fitch's B–) is warranted given the positive trajectory, though further upgrades into B2 territory require structural security improvement and confirmed medium-term growth acceleration above 3%.

> **We assign Ecuador an implied rating of B3 (Moody's equivalent) with a Stable outlook, reflecting IMF-anchored fiscal consolidation and market re-access offset by dollarization constraints, a security crisis, and serial default legacy.**
