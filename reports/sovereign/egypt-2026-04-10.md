## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **Primary surplus on track for FY2025/26 target**: Egypt achieved a primary surplus of +3.5% of GDP in FY2024/25, and the first four months of FY2025/26 show the primary surplus at 1.1% of GDP (up from 0.7% a year earlier), keeping the full-year target of +4.8% within reach. The FY2026/27 budget targets +5.0% primary surplus, reflecting embedded fiscal consolidation that covers the adverse real interest rate–growth differential.
- **Reserve accumulation continues**: Net international reserves rose to **$52.83bn** (March 2026) from $51.5bn in December 2025, representing **6.9 months of import cover** as of January 2026 (CBE Governor). This materially exceeds the IMF's adequacy threshold and signals continued confidence from non-resident carry-trade investors.
- **IMF program formally completed and extended**: The 5th and 6th EFF reviews were formally approved by the IMF Executive Board on **February 26, 2026**, unlocking ~$2.3bn; the RSF first review also cleared. The EFF has been extended, providing a continued multilateral anchor. Structural reform compliance, while uneven, has not triggered program suspension.
- **GDP momentum remains above-trend**: Real GDP growth hit **5.3% in Q1 FY2025/26**, ahead of the IMF's full-year forecast of 4.5–4.7%. The IMF projects 5.4% for FY2026/27 (IMF WEO, January 2026). Growth is broad-based across tourism, manufacturing, and financial services.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Inflation reversal is a material setback**: After falling to 10.1% in December 2025, headline CPI **reversed sharply to 11.9% in January 2026 and 13.4% in February 2026** — the fastest monthly increase since February 2024. Core inflation also rose to 12.7% (February 2026). This reversal jeopardizes the CBE's 5–9% Q4 2026 inflation target and has halted the rate-cutting cycle: the CBE held rates at **19%** on April 2, 2026, citing regional conflict and price pressure.
- **Interest burden remains structurally extreme**: Interest payments consume **87% of tax revenues** and exceed 10% of GDP — among the highest globally for any rated sovereign. The modest rate cuts achieved (200 bps cumulative from 21% to 19%) have not materially relieved this burden. Domestic borrowing costs continue to vastly exceed nominal growth, requiring the full primary surplus just to stabilize debt.
- **Privatization pipeline near-stalled**: Egypt has delivered only **~$420mn** in asset sales (Gebel El Zeit wind farm) under the privatization program, against a multi-billion-dollar annual commitment. Electricity subsidy reform has also been paused. The IMF's 5th/6th review report explicitly noted that "progress on structural reforms has been uneven" and "the divestment agenda has been slower than envisaged," which is the principal source of program conditionality risk.
- **Gross public debt elevated**: General government debt at **~85.6% of GDP** (June 2025), declining from 96% in 2023, but still far above the IMF's 60% low-risk benchmark. Egypt faces external debt maturities of up to **7% of external debt annually in 2026 and 2027**, creating significant refinancing pressure in a higher-for-longer global rate environment.
- **Suez Canal revenue suppression persists**: Canal revenues remain depressed from the 45.5% decline in FY2024/25 due to Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping. Any normalization of traffic — estimated to add **~$8bn** annually — has not yet materialized, and the regional conflict cited by the CBE in April 2026 as a reason to pause rate cuts signals ongoing security risks that directly threaten this revenue stream.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Egypt's gross public debt/GDP has declined from ~96% (June 2023) to ~85.6% (June 2025), and the IMF baseline projects continued reduction toward 75–80% by 2028–2030, contingent on maintaining the 4–5% primary surplus target. The **r–g differential** remains structurally adverse: with the CBE policy rate at 19% and average domestic borrowing costs well above nominal GDP growth (~4.7% real + ~13% inflation implied nominal ≈ 18% nominal GDP), Egypt is in a narrow equilibrium where the primary surplus just offsets the snowball effect. Any revenue shortfall, subsidy-reversal backtracking, or growth disappointment below 4% would see debt stabilization stall or reverse. The IMF's extension of the EFF and the FY2026/27 budget targeting a 5.0% primary surplus provide some buffer, but the arithmetic leaves no room for fiscal slippage.

### 3C. External Liquidity

Reserves of $52.83bn (March 2026) covering 6.9 months of import cover comfortably exceed IMF adequacy thresholds, and the CBE reports reserve coverage of ~158% of short-term external debt. However, as noted in prior analysis, an estimated $25–30bn of reserves reflects non-resident EGP T-bill carry-trade positions (yielding ~19% on a short-duration basis), which are highly reversible in risk-off conditions; the structural reserve cushion net of hot money is materially thinner than the headline figure. External debt maturities of **~7% of total external debt annually in 2026 and 2027** (~$11–12bn per year on a $163.7bn base) represent significant rollover pressure that the reserve buffer and bilateral GCC support lines are designed to absorb, but leave limited margin for simultaneous portfolio outflows and trade-financing stress.

**Willingness-to-Pay Synthesis:** Egypt's external debt service track record is strong and multi-crisis-tested — the key constraint is the military's structural resistance to privatization, which is the most significant unresolved tension in the IMF program. The April 2026 CBE rate hold amid regional conflict is a defensible monetary policy decision, but the re-acceleration of inflation to 13.4% introduces execution uncertainty around the central bank's 5–9% Q4 2026 target that was not present in the February 2026 analysis.

## 7. ESG CONSIDERATIONS

| ESG Factor | Assessment |
|------------|------------|
| **E – Environmental** | Egypt faces acute water scarcity risk driven by the Nile basin dynamics and rising Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD) tensions; the Nile provides ~95% of Egypt's freshwater for a 106 million population. Extreme heat vulnerability threatens agricultural output, which employs ~25% of the workforce. Suez Canal physical infrastructure creates climate exposure (water level changes affect transit capacity). **Credit-relevance:** GERD-related water stress creates contingent fiscal liability (food import subsidies, agricultural support programs) and is a live geopolitical risk vector with Ethiopia that could escalate to conflict affecting regime stability. Partial energy subsidy unwinding exposes consumers to commodity volatility. |
| **S – Social** | Egypt's middle class remains under sustained purchasing-power pressure after the hyperinflationary shock of 2023–24; the February 2026 CPI re-acceleration to 13.4% will deepen this stress. Population of ~106 million growing at ~1.7% annually creates persistent labor market absorption demands. Youth unemployment is structurally elevated, and the US State Department Investment Climate Statement (September 2025) documented significant constraints on civil society and labor rights. **Credit-relevance:** Social instability risk is underpriced by markets — the authoritarian stability premium Egypt currently trades on is historically fragile to economic shocks, as demonstrated in 2011; a sustained cost-of-living crisis without visible distributional relief could catalyze popular pressure on the regime in ways that would materially affect sovereign risk. |
| **G – Governance** | WGI 2024 revision: Voice & Accountability ~10th percentile; Rule of Law ~30th percentile; Political Stability ~25th percentile; Control of Corruption ~25th percentile; Government Effectiveness ~35th percentile. The Egyptian military controls an estimated 25–40% of the formal non-oil economy through tax-advantaged, opaque conglomerates. The February 2026 MEES assessment ("Privatization Advances, Subsidy Reform Stalls") confirms the structural pattern: cosmetic compliance on some privatization benchmarks while core military enterprises remain shielded. **Credit-relevance:** Military economic entrenchment is the single most significant structural ceiling on Egypt's credit profile — it limits privatization pace (core IMF conditionality), depresses private sector FDI, and creates a regime-survival calculus that periodically overrides economic optimization. This feature is not addressable through rating upgrades until verifiable and material privatization of military-economy assets is demonstrated. |

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Inflation fails to return below **11%** by Q3 2026, forcing CBE to reverse rate cuts or triggering a renewed EGP depreciation episode and reserve drawdown below **5 months of import cover**
- Primary fiscal balance slips below **+2.5% of GDP** due to subsidy reversal reversal, electoral spending, or energy sector fiscal support expansion
- **Suez Canal revenues collapse further** (>30% additional decline) from renewed or expanded Red Sea conflict, removing $3–5bn from current account receipts
- Non-resident T-bill carry-trade exit (currently ~$25–30bn of the $52.83bn reserve base) overwhelms reserve buffer and triggers disorderly EGP depreciation
- IMF program extension after Autumn 2026 fails to reach Board agreement due to structural conditionality non-compliance on privatization
- External debt service stress: $11–12bn annual maturities in 2026–2027 combined with capital outflows exceed available reserve and bilateral financing capacity

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Inflation returns durably below **9%** (CBE target midpoint) by Q3 2026, enabling the resumption of the rate-cutting cycle and meaningful reduction in domestic interest burden
- Primary surplus maintained above **4.5% of GDP** for two consecutive fiscal years, placing gross debt/GDP on a **confirmed declining path to below 80% by 2028**
- Privatization pipeline generates **>$5bn in FY2025/26** and demonstrates credible pipeline for FY2026/27, including sale of at least one military-economy enterprise, validating IMF structural conditionality compliance
- Suez Canal revenues normalize fully (**>$10bn annually**) on sustained basis, materially improving the current account balance toward the −3% benchmark

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Egypt's macroeconomic stabilization since March 2024 represents a genuine and consequential reform achievement: the country has navigated the IMF program through six consecutive reviews without suspension, rebuilt reserves from near-crisis levels to $52.83bn (6.9 months import cover), and sustained a primary surplus of 3.5% of GDP — extraordinary for a B-rated sovereign. The formal completion of the 5th and 6th EFF reviews on February 26, 2026 removes a near-term program risk and the GDP growth beat in Q1 FY2025/26 (5.3%) provides some buffer to the macro outlook. However, the February 2026 CPI re-acceleration to 13.4% — reversing from 10.1% just two months earlier — is a genuine credit event: it has paused the CBE easing cycle (rate hold, April 2, 2026), threatens the central bank's Q4 2026 inflation target, and introduces uncertainty around the disinflation narrative that underpinned the prior upgrade cycle. Combined with a near-stalled privatization program (only $420mn delivered), persistent Suez Canal revenue depression, and the structural interest-to-tax-revenue ratio of 87%, our B3 implied rating continues to reflect a sovereign navigating a narrow macroeconomic equilibrium with minimal margin for error. The Positive outlook is retained on the basis that the fiscal consolidation framework remains intact and the Moody's trajectory is clearly toward convergence with S&P/Fitch's B, but the upgrade timeline has extended to 12–18 months contingent on demonstrated disinflation resumption and privatization delivery.

> **We assign Egypt an implied rating of B3 (Moody's equivalent) with a Positive outlook, reflecting intact fiscal consolidation and reserve rebuild offset by the inflation reversal, stalled privatization, and structural interest burden that leave no margin for policy slippage.**
