## §1 RATING ACTION & OUTLOOK

| Sovereign | Report Date | Implied Rating | Outlook | Moody's / Fitch / S&P | Action |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|---------|----------------------|--------|
| Colombia | 2026-05-03 | BB+ | Negative | Baa3 (stable) / BB (stable) / BB (negative) | **Affirm BB+, Negative Outlook** — Repeated fiscal slippages, fiscal rule suspension, and record-high external debt sustain downward pressure on a crossover credit stranded at the investment-grade boundary. |

**Prior ratings:** Moody's Baa2 → Baa3 (downgrade Jun-2025); S&P BB+ → BB (downgrade Jun-2025); Fitch BB+ → BB (downgrade Dec-2025).

Colombia occupies a precarious crossover position: Moody's alone retains the country in investment grade at Baa3 (stable), while both S&P and Fitch have moved to speculative grade at BB following consecutive fiscal underperformances and the temporary suspension of the fiscal rule — the cornerstone of macroeconomic credibility enshrined since 2011. The IMF's 2025 Article IV assessment (Sep-29, 2025) explicitly noted that fiscal policy and the policy framework have "deteriorated" since the 2024 Flexible Credit Line (FCL) review, with gross public debt projected to exceed 61% of GDP through the medium term, well above the 55% statutory debt anchor.

The Negative Outlook reflects the absence of a credible, detailed medium-term fiscal consolidation path. The 2026 draft budget targets an overall deficit of 6.2% of GDP and a primary deficit of 2.0% of GDP — providing no meaningful debt-stabilizing consolidation in the near term. With BanRep's policy rate raised to 11.25% in April 2026 in response to resurgent inflation (5.1% end-2025, projected 6.3% in 2026), the interest burden on public debt continues to widen. Failure to implement structural consolidation measures in the 2026-2028 window risks full convergence to speculative grade across all three agencies.

## §2 KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Strength 1 — Substantial FX Reserve Buffer and Market Access**
Colombia maintains international reserves of ~USD 66.1 billion as of end-November 2025 (Banrep), equivalent to approximately 9.9 months of imports. The IMF's ARA composite metric stood at 1.30 (May 2025), 30% above the adequacy threshold. Inclusion in JPMorgan GBI-EM and EMBI indices ensures broad institutional investor access. The EMBI spread of 282 bps (end-Q1 2026, JPMorgan) remains below the Latin American regional average of 308 bps, reflecting residual market confidence despite the fiscal deterioration.

**Strength 2 — Positive Growth Momentum and BanRep Independence**
Real GDP growth reached ~3.4% year-on-year in Q3 2025 (Banrep), with the IMF projecting 2.5% for full-year 2025 and 2.3% for 2026 (IMF WEO Apr-2026). Banco de la República has demonstrated robust operational independence, raising the policy rate aggressively from 9.25% (Nov-2025) to 11.25% (Apr-2026) despite political economy pressures in a pre-electoral environment. The inflation-targeting mandate is legally enshrined, and the BanRep board's composition is insulated from executive interference — a key structural rating support.

**Strength 3 — Unblemished Debt Service Record and FCL Track Record**
Colombia has no history of sovereign default or restructuring. All external obligations have been serviced on time through multiple commodity cycles, the 2008 global financial crisis, and the Covid-19 shock. The IMF Flexible Credit Line arrangement — maintained continuously since 2009 — is an unmatched institutional credential in the EM universe, reflecting a historically predictable macroeconomic policy framework even as current fiscal slippage represents a qualitative departure from that track record.

**Constraint 1 — Fiscal Rule Suspension and Chronic Primary Deficit**
The temporary suspension of Colombia's fiscal rule is the most severe near-term rating constraint. Gross public debt rose to 61.2% of GDP by end-2024, exceeding the 55% debt anchor by 6 percentage points (IMF Art. IV Sep-2025). The 2026 budget targets a primary deficit of 2.0% of GDP with no credible path to surplus. The IMF explicitly downgraded its fiscal policy assessment from "very strong" to below FCL qualification standard. The medium-term fiscal framework targets a return to the rule deficit target by 2028 but "falls short of providing details on the underlying measures" (IMF Art. IV, 2025).

**Constraint 2 — Resurgent Inflation and Rising Interest Burden**
Inflation reversed its 2024 downward trend, reaching 5.1% by end-2025 (core inflation 5.0%), and is projected to rise to 6.3% in 2026 before declining toward the 3.0% target by early 2027 at earliest (Banrep/BBVA Research). BanRep's defensive rate hike cycle to 11.25% materially increases the government's domestic debt servicing costs, crowding out expenditure flexibility. Net central government debt is projected at 63% of GDP by end-2028 — 7 percentage points above last year's MTFF projection — illustrating a pattern of systematic fiscal forecast overoptimism.

**Constraint 3 — Record External Debt and Widening Current Account Deficit**
External debt reached a record USD 246.8 billion (53.8% of GDP) at end-2025, up from 52.7% a year earlier (Banrep/DANE, Mar-2026), with the public sector accounting for USD 152.7 billion. The current account deficit is projected to widen from -1.7% of GDP (2024A, its lowest in 20 years) to -2.5% (2025E) and -3.3% (2026F) as terms of trade deteriorate and domestic demand expands (IMF Art. IV). External FX debt exposure above 50% of GDP breaches standard DSA benchmark thresholds and concentrates vulnerability to COP depreciation shocks.

## §3B DEBT TRAJECTORY

Gross public debt rose to 61.2% of GDP by end-2024 and is projected to remain near or above 62% through end-2025, driven by a primary deficit of ~2.0% of GDP combined with real growth (~2.3%) insufficient to outpace interest accumulation (IMF CR No. 25/280, Sep-2025). The interest-growth differential has turned adverse: with domestic borrowing costs rising on BanRep's rate hike cycle (policy rate 11.25% in Apr-2026) and real growth anchored well below historic potential, the stabilizing primary balance requirement likely exceeds -0.5% of GDP — a threshold Colombia has not approached since fiscal framework deterioration began in 2022. The MTFF targets substantial consolidation across 2026-2028 and a return to the fiscal rule deficit target by 2028, but IMF Directors explicitly flagged that the plan lacks specific operational measures — a critical credibility gap that markets and rating agencies have already priced.

Colombia's external debt amortization profile provides some near-term relief: 86.1% of the USD 246.8 billion external stock carries maturities longer than one year, with short-term obligations at 13.9% (Banrep, end-2025). However, the public sector's USD 152.7 billion external borrowing — comprising USD-denominated TES, euro bonds, and multilateral loans — concentrates refinancing risk in global capital markets, where re-pricing in a higher-for-longer rate environment is non-trivial. IMF projections show net central government debt reaching 63% of GDP by end-2028, 7 percentage points above last year's MTFF forecast — a recurring pattern of fiscal optimism bias that compounds credibility erosion. Under a growth underperformance scenario (growth at 1.5% vs. 2.3% baseline), the debt stock could reach 67-70% of GDP by 2028, pushing Colombia firmly into the speculative-grade peer cluster. Conditional sustainability — the IMF's baseline assessment that debt "remains sustainable conditional on the assumed fiscal consolidation" — is a fragile anchor given three years of successive policy slippage.

## §3C EXTERNAL LIQUIDITY

Colombia's external liquidity profile remains a primary rating support. International reserves of ~USD 66.1 billion (Nov-2025, Banrep) cover approximately 9.9 months of imports, well above the 3-month minimum and the 5-month EM convention. The IMF's ARA composite metric at 1.30 (May 2025) confirms reserves exceed the adequate level by 30%, providing buffer against capital outflow shocks, export shortfalls, and broad money volatility. The precautionary Flexible Credit Line (~$6.5 billion) augments available liquidity, though its qualification status is under formal review following the fiscal policy downgrade in the Sep-2025 Article IV assessment.

External financing requirements are nevertheless rising. The current account deficit is forecast at -3.3% of GDP in 2026 (IMF Art. IV), primarily financed by FDI rather than portfolio flows — a structural differentiator that reduces sudden-stop vulnerability relative to peers dependent on hot-money inflows. However, the gross external financing requirement (current account deficit plus medium- and long-term external amortizations) is estimated to exceed 15% of GDP in 2026, a level that warrants monitoring. Colombia's export concentration in hydrocarbons (~40% of goods exports) creates a triple-hit vulnerability in an oil price shock scenario: fiscal revenue compression, current account widening, and COP depreciation would simultaneously worsen all three components of the external debt-to-GDP ratio. The FX reserve buffer is robust but would erode rapidly if deployed to defend structural, rather than cyclical, balance-of-payments imbalances.

The pre-electoral 2026 environment compounds risk: political incentives favor expenditure expansion over structural consolidation. Composite willingness-to-pay assessment: **3.5/5** — high technical capacity and intent to service obligations, materially diminished capacity to credibly commit to fiscal adjustment.

Colombia trades at a spread premium to Brazil (282 vs. ~230 bps) despite a materially lower debt-to-GDP ratio (~62% vs. ~89%), reflecting the market's acute pricing of fiscal credibility risk — the fiscal rule suspension and electoral spending uncertainty penalize the spread beyond what debt levels alone would imply. Against Peru, Colombia's structural fiscal and governance gaps are stark: Peru's ~34% debt/GDP provides vast fiscal headroom, while Colombia's 62% debt at comparable growth rates exposes the asymmetry of fiscal discipline. Panama is the closest structural peer — both are crossover Baa3/BB credits with similar CA deficits and growth profiles, trading at similar spreads (~243 vs. 282 bps) — but Panama's dollarized economy, Canal revenue base, and stronger rule-of-law institutions provide a structural anchor absent in Colombia. The 40 bps spread differential fairly prices this governance gap.

## §8 RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Downgrade Triggers:**
- Gross public debt exceeds 65% of GDP on a sustained basis without a credible IMF-validated reversal path, signaling full speculative-grade convergence and potential FCL revocation
- Primary deficit remains at or widens beyond -2.5% of GDP in the 2026 or 2027 budget cycles, confirming collapse of the MTFF consolidation trajectory
- EMBI spread sustainably above 400 bps for two consecutive quarters, signaling loss of voluntary market access at sustainable borrowing costs

**Upgrade Triggers:**
- Credible structural revenue reform passed into law, IMF-verified, placing the primary balance on a trajectory to near-zero by 2027, with BanRep confirming inflation convergence to target
- Fiscal rule reinstated with constitutional or supralegislative backing and a minimum two-year track record of outperformance vs. MTFF targets under independent audit
- Gross public debt/GDP trajectory confirmed declining toward 57-58% of GDP in two successive IMF DSA baseline assessments, restoring the debt anchor buffer

---

## §9 ANALYST CONCLUSION

Colombia's sovereign credit stands at a genuine inflection point. The country's macroeconomic buffers — reserve adequacy, FDI-financed external deficit, an independent and credible central bank, and an unblemished debt service record — continue to provide a floor for Moody's residual investment-grade assessment at Baa3. But the qualitative deterioration of the fiscal framework is now the dominant and accelerating rating driver: the suspension of the fiscal rule, three consecutive years of primary deficit overruns against MTFF targets, and a 2026 consolidation plan that lacks operational specificity represent a systematic erosion of the institutional anchor that historically justified Colombia's premium over regional peers. With S&P and Fitch already at BB and Moody's at the last rung of investment grade, Colombia is a convergence risk — the probability of full speculative-grade consensus across all three agencies within 12 to 18 months is non-trivial absent decisive fiscal action in the 2026 electoral cycle, where political incentives structurally favor the downside.

> **We assign Colombia an implied rating of BB+ with a Negative Outlook, reflecting the country's precarious crossover position between Moody's residual investment-grade assessment (Baa3/stable) and the speculative-grade consensus of S&P and Fitch (BB/negative and BB/stable), as chronic fiscal slippages, the suspension of the fiscal rule anchor, and record-high external debt at 53.8% of GDP systematically outweigh the support provided by ~9.9 months of import coverage in FX reserves, BanRep's monetary credibility, and an unblemished debt service history dating to modern independence.**
