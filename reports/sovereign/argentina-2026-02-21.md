## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **Fiscal anchor holding**: Argentina posted a primary surplus of **+1.8% of GDP in 2024** and **+1.4% of GDP in 2025** — the first back-to-back primary surpluses since 2008 — outperforming IMF EFF program targets. A 2026 budget targets **+1.2–1.5% primary surplus**.
- **IMF EFF $20bn anchor**: The 48-month EFF (April 2025, $20bn total), with an initial $12bn disbursement, provides a durable financing backstop. The 1st review (July 2025) confirmed met performance criteria; the program reduces rollover risk through 2029.
- **Electoral mandate reinforced**: Milei's LLA captured **40.8% of the vote** in October 2025 midterms, nearly tripling congressional seats to ~one-third of the Chamber — reducing legislative gridlock risk and increasing structural reform execution probability.
- **Reserves accumulating**: Gross international reserves reached **USD 45.3 billion** (February 2026), the highest since 2021 and +$11.6bn vs. end-2024, as BCRA maintained active FX purchases in the managed float band.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Inflation structurally elevated**: CPI inflation stands at **32.4% y/y** (January 2026), one of the highest rates globally; while dramatically lower than the 200%+ peak in late 2023, disinflation progress has slowed, and risks of wage-price spirals persist.
- **Serial default history**: Three sovereign defaults in 25 years (2001, 2014, 2020); willingness-to-pay assessments remain structurally discounted. Any policy reversal or loss of market access would immediately re-price risk sharply.
- **Exchange rate band fragility**: The January 2026 crawling band regime (ARS 1,000–1,400/USD, expanding 2.5–2.8%/month) introduces FX uncertainty; a band breach would destabilize inflation expectations and IMF program compliance, as occurred in October 2025.
- **Gross debt burden very high**: General government gross debt/GDP at **~85%** (2024), though this includes significant intra-public sector holdings; net public debt (~52% of GDP) is more representative but still elevated.
- **Current account swinging to deficit**: CA balance deteriorated from a +1% surplus (2024) to an estimated **−2.0% deficit** (2025) and projected **−2.5%** (2026) as imports surged; rising external financing needs amplify vulnerability to market sentiment shocks.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Argentina's **net public debt/GDP** is projected to decline from ~52% (2024) to ~31% by 2030 under the IMF baseline, driven by the interaction of primary surpluses (averaging +1.5% GDP) and high nominal GDP growth (real growth of ~4% + inflation of 20–32%) that mechanically erodes peso-denominated liabilities. The **r–g dynamic** on USD-denominated external debt (~43% of total) remains adverse — USD borrowing costs of ~8–9% against real growth of 4% require a sustained primary surplus of ~2% of GDP to prevent USD debt from rebounding — a condition currently met but dependent on program continuity. On the peso debt stock, nominal GDP growth acts as a powerful deleveraging mechanism, explaining why IMF net debt projections improve rapidly despite the gross debt headline.

### 3C. External Liquidity

Gross reserves of USD 45.3 billion (February 2026) represent the highest level of the Milei administration, boosted by active BCRA FX purchases and IMF disbursements; however, **net international reserves** — after netting out IMF obligations (~5% of GDP due in 2025–2027), FX swap lines, and short-term obligations — remain substantially lower, estimated at USD 10–15 billion net, which constrains the effective liquidity buffer. Argentina faces USD 10 billion in Treasury and BCRA FX maturities between August 2025 and January 2026, and the exchange rate band's integrity depends on maintaining credible reserve accumulation; any reversal in BCRA purchasing stance would quickly tighten the effective net reserve cushion.

## 4. WILLINGNESS TO PAY

| Dimension | Score (1–5) | Commentary |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Governance & Rule of Law (WGI) | **3** | Argentina ranks in the ~40th percentile for Rule of Law and ~45th percentile for Government Effectiveness (WGI 2023) — better than regional peers at similar rating levels; Milei administration has strengthened institutional commitment to rules-based fiscal framework |
| Fiscal Policy Credibility | **4** | Two consecutive primary surpluses (2024–2025) represent a structural break from 15 years of deficits; the zero-deficit commitment is politically embedded and supported by congressional majority; 2026 budget target upheld despite social pressure |
| Central Bank Independence | **3** | BCRA has operated without monetary financing since program inception; crawling band regime limits full independence, but disciplined FX intervention and reserve accumulation demonstrate operational credibility; inflation still structurally high |
| Debt Payment Track Record | **1** | Three defaults in 25 years (2001, 2014, 2020); each restructuring involved significant holdouts or haircuts; willingness-to-pay is the core credit constraint and requires multi-year track record to materially re-rate |

**Willingness-to-Pay Synthesis:** The Milei administration has demonstrated unprecedented fiscal discipline for Argentina — the zero-deficit rule has been maintained through social protests, a nationwide general strike (February 19, 2026), and contentious labor reform negotiations. The IMF EFF 1st review passed with met QPCs, and the program's political anchor is reinforced by U.S. backing (Trump-era $20bn swap line + loan facility). Nonetheless, Argentina's three defaults in 25 years represent the most acute willingness-to-pay constraint in this rating cohort; creditors require a sustained multi-year track record — not yet achieved — before willingness-to-pay scores can be materially upgraded. A successor government's willingness to honor current commitments remains the existential tail risk.

## 7. ESG CONSIDERATIONS

| ESG Factor | Assessment |
|------------|------------|
| **E – Environmental** | Argentina is a major agricultural exporter (soybeans, corn, beef), making it highly exposed to La Niña drought cycles that reduce export revenues and fiscal receipts (soy export taxes are a key budget component, ~1–2% of GDP). Lithium-rich Andean provinces (Jujuy, Salta) represent a critical green-transition asset but require massive infrastructure investment and face indigenous rights disputes. **Credit-relevant:** Agricultural weather shocks directly compress fiscal revenues and current account, creating procyclical fiscal vulnerability; lithium upside provides long-term revenue diversification potential. |
| **S – Social** | Poverty rate spiked to ~50% of population in early 2024 (driven by hyperinflationary shock), recovering gradually as real wages recover in 2025. Gini coefficient ~42. Nationwide general strike (February 19, 2026) organized by Argentina's largest labor unions (CGT, CTA) reflects deep social discontent with Milei's austerity agenda, including pension reform and public sector layoffs. **Credit-relevant:** Social instability constrains the pace and depth of structural fiscal reform; a sustained social backlash or political shift could reverse the primary surplus achievement within one budget cycle. |
| **G – Governance** | WGI 2023: Rule of Law ~40th percentile; Government Effectiveness ~45th percentile; Control of Corruption ~38th percentile. Milei administration has introduced a more rules-based fiscal framework than predecessors, with zero-deficit commitment enforced through vetoes and executive orders. However, presidential decree reliance to circumvent congress (DNU mechanisms) raises institutional durability concerns. **Credit-relevant:** Governance quality above regional peers at this rating level is a relative strength; but the reform program's dependence on presidential leadership rather than legislative institutionalization creates succession risk and limits the permanence premium creditors can assign. |

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Primary fiscal balance slips to **deficit within 12 months** (zero-deficit rule abandoned), triggering IMF program suspension and loss of market access
- Exchange rate band **breached or abandoned** under reserve pressure, re-igniting inflation above 50% y/y and derailing disinflation trajectory
- Net international reserves fall below **USD 5 billion** (critically thin given USD debt service schedule), triggering speculative attack
- Successor government elected in 2027 **reverses fiscal anchor** or defaults selectively on restructured 2020 debt obligations — fourth default in 25 years

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Inflation decelerates durably to **below 20% y/y by December 2026**, signaling monetary credibility restoration and enabling interest rate normalization
- Primary surplus maintained at **+1.5% GDP or above** for two consecutive fiscal years (2025 and 2026), demonstrating reform durability through electoral cycle
- Net international reserves build to **above USD 20 billion** on a sustained basis, materially reducing rollover risk and enabling FX band exit toward managed float
- Structural reforms (labor market, pension system) passed with congressional majority, reducing long-term fiscal rigidity and enabling FDI inflows to GDP growth above 5%

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Argentina represents the most dramatic sovereign credit transformation in the current EM landscape. Under President Milei, the country has achieved something historically improbable: two consecutive primary fiscal surpluses (2024: +1.8% GDP; 2025: +1.4% GDP) after 15 years of chronic deficits, against a backdrop of inflation inherited at 200%+ that has since decelerated to 32% y/y — still extremely elevated, but directionally correct. The $20 billion IMF EFF (April 2025) — with $14 billion disbursed through the 1st review — provides the external anchor that prior programs lacked, while Milei's October 2025 midterm landslide (40.8% of vote, ~1/3 of Chamber) materially reduces legislative risk for the remainder of the reform program. The exchange rate band introduced in January 2026 (ARS 1,000–1,400/USD) represents a meaningful step toward monetary normalization, though its credibility depends on sustained reserve accumulation. The key risks — serial default legacy (three in 25 years), structurally elevated inflation, and the current account swinging into deficit as domestic demand recovers — are real and anchor the current Caa1 rating. However, the reform amplitude and pace place Argentina on a credible upgrade trajectory, and continued IMF program compliance through 2026 would likely justify a one-notch upgrade to B3 by year-end.

> **We assign Argentina an implied rating of Caa1 with a Positive outlook, reflecting unprecedented fiscal consolidation and IMF-anchored reform offset by serial default legacy, elevated inflation, and exchange rate band fragility.**
