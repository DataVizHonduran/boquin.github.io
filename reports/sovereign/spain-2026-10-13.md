## §1 Rating Action & Outlook

| Sovereign | Date | Implied Rating | Outlook | Prior Ratings (Moody's/Fitch/S&P) | Action |

| Spain | 2026-10-13 | **A/A3** | Stable | A3 (Moody's, Stable) / A (Fitch, Stable) / A+ (S&P, Stable) | Affirm, following a multi-notch upgrade cycle — Spain is the strongest growth and upgrade story among the four largest eurozone sovereigns. |

Spain enters late 2026 as the best-performing large eurozone credit story: Moody's upgraded to A3 from Baa1 and Fitch to A from A- (both 2025-09-26), following S&P's move to A+ (2025-09-12), all with Stable outlooks [[sources below]]. The upgrades reflect genuinely differentiated growth — 3.5% in 2024, 2.8% in 2025, and an IMF-upgraded 2.5% forecast for 2026, versus a euro-area average near 1.1% — alongside a narrowing fiscal deficit (2.4% of GDP in 2025) and a declining debt/GDP trajectory from a still-elevated 101.8% base (end-2024).

## §2 Key Rating Drivers

**Strengths**

- **Standout growth differentiation.** The IMF raised its 2026 Spain growth forecast to 2.5%, more than double the euro-area average and well above Germany (1.3%), Italy (0.8%) and France (0.4%) on the same IMF comparison — a structural outperformance driven by immigration-fueled labor force growth and strong services exports.
- **Improving fiscal position.** The deficit narrowed to 2.4% of GDP in 2025 from 3.1% in 2024, with strong tax-revenue growth from the economic expansion directly funding the consolidation (IMF 2026 Article IV, concluded 2026-05-22).
- **Declining debt trajectory.** IMF staff project debt/GDP continuing to decline over the next five years from the end-2024 base of 101.8% of GDP, aided by nominal growth running well ahead of financing costs.

**Constraints**

- **Still-elevated absolute debt level.** Despite the improving trajectory, debt/GDP above 100% leaves Spain vulnerable to growth or financing-cost shocks, and the IMF explicitly flags gross financing needs remaining "slightly above 14 percent" of GDP through 2030.
- **Sticky inflation.** Headline inflation is revised up for 2025-2026 (Banco de España, December 2025 projections: 2.7% in 2025, 2.1%+ in 2026, with some more recent estimates running materially higher amid Middle East-war energy spillovers), driven by persistent core/services price pressure.
- **Medium-term aging/pension pressure.** The IMF and OECD both flag "looming spending pressures" from pension and aging-related expenditure as a structural medium-term fiscal risk that could offset near-term consolidation gains.

## §3B Debt Trajectory

Spain's debt trajectory is the clearest positive-momentum story among the four large eurozone sovereigns. From a debt/GDP level of 101.8% at end-2024, the IMF's 2025 and 2026 Article IV consultations both project continued decline over the following five years, driven by the combination of above-average nominal growth (real GDP growth of 2.8% in 2025, forecast at 2.5% in 2026) and a narrowing fiscal deficit (2.4% of GDP in 2025, down from 3.1% in 2024). The arithmetic is favorable: with nominal growth running well ahead of Spain's financing costs (10Y yield ~4.12-4.13%, among the tightest spreads to Bunds of any non-AAA eurozone sovereign), the growth-interest differential is actively working to reduce the debt ratio even before any further primary-balance improvement.

The IMF nonetheless characterizes medium-term debt risk as "moderate" rather than low, citing gross financing needs projected to remain slightly above 14% of GDP and debt near 93% of GDP by 2030 — still a meaningful absolute burden. The principal medium-term risk flagged by both the IMF and the OECD's 2025 Economic Survey is "looming spending pressures," specifically pension and aging-related costs, which could erode the current favorable trajectory if not addressed through further structural reform.

## §3C External Liquidity

Spain's external accounts have strengthened into a structural surplus. The current account balance ran at 2.3% of GDP in 2025, improving to a forecast 2.5% in 2026, reflecting robust services exports (notably tourism) and the broader economic expansion. As a euro-area member, Spain has no independent reserve-adequacy constraint in the EM sense; reserve and FX backstop functions sit with the Eurosystem.

Gross external debt stood at 162% of GDP (Q3-2025, Trading Economics), a whole-economy figure including banking and corporate cross-border liabilities, while net external debt — netting out Spain's external assets — was a more moderate 44.2% of GDP (September 2025). Combined with a current account surplus, this indicates Spain's external financing requirement is well covered by ongoing current receipts, and the gap between gross and net external debt (162% vs. 44%) underscores that headline gross figures substantially overstate the sovereign-relevant external vulnerability.

## §8 Rating Sensitivities

**Downgrade triggers**
- Growth decelerates sharply below the IMF's 1.8% 2027 baseline, undermining the debt-decline trajectory
- Pension/aging spending pressures materialize without offsetting reform, reversing fiscal consolidation gains
- Gross financing needs rise materially above the IMF's ~14% of GDP medium-term projection

**Upgrade triggers**
- Debt/GDP decline accelerates below the IMF's 93%-by-2030 baseline
- Further structural reform addressing pension sustainability is legislated and credible
- Current account surplus and growth outperformance versus euro-area peers persist through 2027-28

## §9 Analyst Conclusion

Spain's credit trajectory is the strongest among the four largest eurozone sovereigns, underpinned by growth running more than double the euro-area average, a narrowing fiscal deficit funded by that growth rather than austerity, and a declining debt/GDP path from an elevated but moderating base. The September 2025 triple-agency upgrade reflects genuine, structurally-grounded improvement rather than a one-off cyclical boost, though the IMF's "moderate" (not low) risk characterization and explicit pension/aging warnings are the key medium-term watch-items.

> **We assign Spain an implied rating of A/A3 with a Stable Outlook, reflecting standout growth and a genuinely improving fiscal and debt trajectory, tempered by a still-elevated absolute debt level and unresolved medium-term pension-related fiscal risk.**
