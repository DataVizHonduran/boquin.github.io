| Item | Detail |

| **Prior Rating** | Moody's: Baa2/Negative; Fitch: BBB/Stable; S&P: BBB/Stable |
| **Action** | **Affirm Baa2 / Maintain Negative Outlook** |

> **Rationale:** Mexico retains investment-grade status anchored by its USMCA trade framework, large reserve buffer (~US$250bn), deep domestic capital markets, and an IMF Flexible Credit Line backstop. However, a deteriorating institutional environment — driven by the elected judiciary reform and accelerated MORENA consolidation of power — fiscal trajectory pointing toward debt rising to 61.5% of GDP by 2030, and subdued growth of only 1.3–1.5% in 2026 (amid US tariff uncertainty) sustain Negative Outlook pressure. The gap between Moody's (Negative) and S&P/Fitch (Stable) reflects genuine analytical uncertainty about whether institutional erosion will materially impair Mexico's creditworthiness over the medium term.

---

## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **USMCA anchor and large reserve buffer:** Mexico's deep integration with the US and Canada under USMCA limits sovereign financing risk; gross FX reserves of ~US$250bn (January 2026) provide a formidable buffer even as month-of-import coverage has declined to 3.4 months (October 2025).
- **Fiscal consolidation underway:** The overall public sector deficit narrowed from 5.7% of GDP in 2024 to 4.3% in 2025, with a primary surplus of ~0.2% achieved; the 2026 budget targets further consolidation to a 0.5% primary surplus and 4.1% overall deficit — indicating a credible, if gradual, adjustment path.
- **Manageable external financing position:** Mexico's external debt is predominantly in FX (~30–35% of total public debt), but the absolute reserve stock and continued access to deep international capital markets provide ample rollover capacity; EMBIG spreads remain at ~175–200 bps, firmly in the investment-grade zone.
- **Credible monetary framework:** Banxico executed eight consecutive rate cuts in 2025, reducing the policy rate from 11% to 7% while keeping January 2026 headline inflation at 3.8% — within the 2–4% target band — demonstrating monetary credibility despite easing.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Institutional erosion from judicial reform:** Mexico became the world's first country to elect its entire judiciary by popular vote; the June 2025 elections returned a Morena-aligned bench with only 13% voter turnout, raising material concerns about judicial independence, rule of law, and the protection of private sector property rights — factors already pressuring foreign direct investment and business confidence.
- **Subdued growth and US tariff risk:** GDP growth is projected at only 1.3–1.5% in 2026 (IMF/World Bank), against a potential growth rate estimated at 2–2.5%; US tariffs — imposed at 25% on non-USMCA goods in early 2025 before partial exemptions — remain a live risk ahead of the 2026 USMCA review, threatening Mexico's export-led growth model.
- **Rising debt trajectory:** IMF gross public sector debt is projected to reach ~59–61% of GDP over the medium term, rising from a recent cyclical low; the interest-to-revenue ratio already stands at a concerning 17% versus a Baa-median of 9%, underscoring fiscal vulnerability.
- **Weak growth–fiscal nexus:** Persistent below-potential growth (Mexico grew only ~1% in 2025) makes fiscal consolidation structurally difficult, as expenditure rigidities and wage pressures from the 13% minimum wage increase constrain fiscal space on both the revenue and spending sides.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Under the IMF's 2025 Article IV baseline, gross public sector debt is projected to rise from ~59% of GDP in 2025 to ~61.5% by 2030, assuming the authorities' fiscal consolidation plan is implemented. The r-g differential is unfavorable: with real interest rates on domestic debt persistently above 3% and real GDP growth running at only 1–1.5%, Mexico requires a sustained primary surplus of at least 1.5% of GDP to stabilize the debt ratio — a target it has not yet achieved. The interest burden of 17% of revenue (vs. a Baa-median of 9%) represents the single most concerning fiscal metric and the primary driver of Moody's Negative Outlook.

### 3C. External Liquidity

Mexico's US$250bn in gross foreign reserves represents one of the largest reserve buffers in emerging markets, providing substantial protection against external shocks. The absolute reserve stock far exceeds short-term external debt obligations and supports continued market access at investment-grade spreads. However, the decline in import coverage from 4.3 to 3.4 months in 2025 — partially reflecting reserve drawdowns to support the peso — warrants monitoring. The IMF Flexible Credit Line (FCL) provides an additional backstop, though Mexico has not drawn on it.

## 4. WILLINGNESS TO PAY

| Dimension | Score (1–5) | Commentary |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Governance & Rule of Law (WGI) | **2.5** | Mexico's WGI scores are in the 30th–40th percentile, significantly impaired by cartel violence, judicial system weaknesses, and corruption perceptions; the elected judiciary reform risks further deterioration in 2026–2027 |
| Fiscal Policy Credibility | **3.5** | Primary surplus achieved in 2025; consolidation plan in place and credible in the near term; however, ambitious growth assumptions embedded in the fiscal plan introduce downside risk |
| Central Bank Independence | **4.0** | Banxico has maintained operational independence through multiple administrations; the 2025 easing cycle has been orderly and data-dependent, with strong credibility in inflation management |
| Debt Payment Track Record | **5.0** | Mexico has an uninterrupted debt service track record spanning several decades; no restructuring events; investment-grade status maintained across multiple economic cycles |

**Synthesis:** Mexico's willingness to pay is not in question — its track record of continuous debt service and strong institutional frameworks at Banxico and the Finance Ministry (SHCP) anchor the rating. The primary concern is **ability** rather than **willingness**: structural growth weakness, institutional erosion through judicial reform, and an unfavorable interest-burden trajectory represent medium-term credit risks rather than near-term default catalysts. The USMCA framework and deep US linkages provide a formidable backstop.

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- USMCA renegotiation fails or US tariffs are permanently applied to USMCA-eligible goods, triggering GDP contraction and external sector deterioration
- Gross public sector debt rises above 65% of GDP on a sustained basis, reflecting structural fiscal adjustment failure
- Judicial reform leads to measurable deterioration in investment protection, triggering FDI outflows and a loss of nearshoring momentum
- Banxico's independence is compromised via political interference, triggering FX depreciation and inflation overshoot
- Primary balance deteriorates back to deficit territory amid spending pressures without growth recovery

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Growth accelerates durably to 2.5%+ driven by nearshoring investment, supporting organic revenue growth and debt stabilization
- Primary surplus sustained above 1.5% of GDP for two or more consecutive years, reversing the debt trajectory
- USMCA successfully renegotiated with enhanced certainty, reducing geopolitical risk premium
- Governance improvements validated by WGI score stabilization and transparent judicial appointment processes

---

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Mexico's sovereign credit profile reflects the tension between structurally sound fundamentals — a USMCA-anchored external sector, formidable reserve position, credible central bank, and unblemished debt service history — and a deteriorating institutional environment that presents the most significant governance credit challenge since the 1994 Tequila crisis. The Sheinbaum administration's elected judiciary represents a structural weakening of rule of law that markets have not yet fully priced, particularly in the context of rising nearshoring-driven FDI expectations. Fiscal consolidation is proceeding, but the pace is insufficient to offset the rising interest burden (17% of revenue) given persistently below-potential growth of 1.0–1.5%. The 2026 USMCA review will be a pivotal credit event: a successful renegotiation would support Outlook stabilization; a breakdown would trigger meaningful downgrade pressure. Mexico retains a meaningful investment-grade credit cushion in the near term, but the medium-term trajectory is uncomfortably one-directional absent structural reforms to boost growth and restore governance credibility.

> **We assign Mexico an implied rating of Baa2 with a Negative Outlook, reflecting strong external buffers and USMCA integration offset by institutional deterioration, a rising debt burden with an unfavorable interest-growth differential, and subdued growth that makes fiscal consolidation structurally challenging.**
