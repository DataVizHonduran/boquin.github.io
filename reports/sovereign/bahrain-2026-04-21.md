| **Action** | **Affirm B2 / Negative Outlook — gross government debt at 134% of GDP and a widening fiscal deficit of 11% of GDP in 2024 sustain negative pressure despite non-hydrocarbon sector resilience and GCC financial backstop** |

> Bahrain's B2/Negative rating reflects an extraordinarily elevated debt burden — 134% of GDP in 2024, the highest in the GCC by a wide margin — combined with a persistent structural fiscal deficit, low foreign exchange reserves, and significant rollover risk on FX-denominated external liabilities. While the peg to the US dollar (BHD 0.376/USD, maintained since 1987) provides monetary credibility, it eliminates exchange rate as an adjustment mechanism and amplifies fiscal financing pressures. The GCC financial support package (USD 10bn pledged in 2018 by Saudi Arabia, UAE, and Kuwait) remains the single most important credit anchor, functioning as a partial first-loss buffer for external creditors.

> The Negative Outlook reflects the absence of a credible medium-term fiscal consolidation path. The IMF's 2025 Article IV (concluded January 23, 2026) explicitly flagged that "additional fiscal measures and reforms are urgently needed to put debt on a durable downward path." Fitch's February 2026 downgrade to B and Moody's April 2026 shift to Negative outlook signal convergent agency concern. An outlook revision to Stable requires demonstrated progress on structural reform — including sustained corporate income tax implementation, subsidy rationalization, and visible debt/GDP stabilization — over a 12–24 month horizon.

## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**

- **GCC Financial Support Backstop**: The 2018 USD 10bn GCC-coordinated support package (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait) materially reduces near-term liquidity tail risk and signals that Bahrain's strategic importance to the Gulf Cooperation Council makes outright default politically costly for the region. This support has underpinned Bahrain's ability to roll external debt at manageable spreads despite deteriorating fundamentals.

- **Non-Hydrocarbon Sector Diversification**: The non-hydrocarbon sector expanded 3.7% in 2024 (IMF, Jan 2026), outpacing the 2.6% headline GDP growth, driven by financial services (Bahrain hosts a significant regional financial hub), tourism, logistics, and digital economy. This diversification — the highest non-oil revenue share among GCC sovereigns — reduces oil-price sensitivity of the growth outlook relative to GCC peers.

- **USD Currency Peg Credibility**: The BHD peg at 0.376/USD, maintained since 1987, provides a monetary anchor and eliminates currency risk for external creditors. Inflation remained subdued at 1.1% (Nov 2025, Trading Economics), consistent with imported price stability. The peg is a fundamental pillar of Bahrain's creditor relations and prevents capital flight dynamics common to high-debt sovereigns.

**Credit Constraints:**

- **Extreme Debt Burden**: Gross government debt reached 134% of GDP in 2024 (IMF, Jan 2026), rising from approximately 100% in 2020 and far above the 60% IMF low-risk benchmark. Fitch projects 136% by 2026, signaling no near-term stabilization. At this level, even moderate interest rate shocks or hydrocarbon price declines translate directly into debt snowball dynamics.

- **Severe Fiscal Deterioration**: The overall fiscal deficit widened to 11% of GDP in 2024 (IMF, Jan 2026), with the government's overdraft at the Central Bank of Bahrain exceeding a quarter of GDP before partial reduction through October 2025. The structural primary balance is estimated at approximately -3% of GDP, requiring significant consolidation to stabilize debt absent exceptional financing from GCC partners.

- **Reserve Inadequacy and Rollover Risk**: FX reserves stood at just over 2 months of prospective non-hydrocarbon imports as of October 2025 (IMF), well below IMF ARA adequacy thresholds and the 3-month benchmark. Combined with Bahrain's reliance on periodic USD Eurobond issuance — with external government debt at BHD 13,841mn (~USD 36.8bn) in 2024 (CEIC) — rollover risk is elevated, particularly under risk-off global conditions.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Bahrain's gross government debt-to-GDP ratio has doubled since 2014, rising from approximately 60% at the time of the oil price collapse to 134% in 2024 — the highest in the GCC by a margin of roughly 3x relative to Oman, the second-most indebted Gulf state. Three interlocking drivers explain the trajectory: (i) persistent structural fiscal deficits averaging 9–11% of GDP since 2020, rooted in high social spending commitments and hydrocarbon revenue volatility; (ii) interest costs estimated at 7–8% of GDP annually given the debt stock and an approximate average borrowing cost of 5.5–6% (consistent with Bahrain's Eurobond coupon history); and (iii) negative real interest rate-growth differentials that sustain snowball dynamics. Fitch projects debt reaching 136% of GDP by 2026 under current policies, with no inflection point absent sustained consolidation. The IMF's 2025 Article IV — though the Staff Report was withheld from publication at authorities' request — explicitly calls for "well-designed and sustained medium-term fiscal consolidation."

Bahrain's debt composition is approximately 60% FX-denominated USD Eurobonds, with the balance held domestically in BHD instruments. Average maturity is estimated at 6–8 years based on recent issuance patterns, but annual gross rollover needs are substantial. A distinct structural risk is the government's overdraft at the CBB, which reached above 25% of GDP in 2024 before an 8% reduction through October 2025 — a quasi-fiscal financing mechanism that the IMF has flagged as a macrofinancial vulnerability creating feedback loops between fiscal and monetary stability. On the positive side, announced fiscal measures — a 15% corporate income tax aligned with the OECD global minimum, energy and utility price reforms — represent the most concrete consolidation signals since the 2018 GCC support package, though their combined revenue contribution is estimated at 1–2% of GDP in the near term, insufficient alone to stabilize debt.

The amortization profile is challenging: several large Eurobond maturities fall in 2025–2028, requiring regular market access at prevailing spreads (~231 bps). GCC bilateral support functions as the de facto refinancing backstop, preventing an outright liquidity crisis. Without this support, Bahrain's debt metrics would be consistent with a substantially lower rating.

### 3C. External Liquidity

The current account remained in surplus at 4.8% of GDP in 2024, supported by hydrocarbon export revenues and a sizeable financial services surplus. However, the surplus has narrowed materially from post-pandemic highs (~9% in 2022) as higher external debt service costs — interest on Eurobonds — and import growth have eroded the position. IMF ARA (Assessing Reserve Adequacy) analysis for a fixed exchange rate regime requires reserves well above the current 2-month level; based on standard ARA methodology (covering short-term debt, portfolio outflows, and trade financing), Bahrain's reserve adequacy gap is estimated at 50–70% of the metric threshold — consistent with the IMF's explicit encouragement to "further strengthen international reserves" in both 2024 and 2025 Article IV communications.

Gross external financing requirements are elevated: combining Eurobond rollovers, interest payments, and incremental current account changes, Bahrain faces estimated gross external financing needs of 15–20% of GDP annually. The USD peg eliminates formal devaluation risk but creates a classic sudden-stop vulnerability — if GCC bilateral support were to falter or market access were to close, the financing gap could not be bridged through exchange rate adjustment. A partial mitigant is the sovereign wealth fund (Future Generations Reserve Fund), with assets estimated at approximately USD 15–18bn (~30–35% of GDP), providing an additional buffer — though these assets are not formally earmarked for debt service and their precise size is not disclosed publicly.

| Debt Payment Track Record | **4/5** | No sovereign default in modern era; Bahrain has met all Eurobond maturities and coupon obligations including through the 2018 stress episode, when GCC support was proactively arranged to prevent market access closure — not a post-default response. **Willingness-to-Pay Synthesis:** Bahrain's payment track record is unblemished, anchored by GCC political economy and demonstrated access to bilateral financial support that differentiates it from typical B-rated peers. Fiscal policy credibility is structurally weak — a decade of target underperformance has entrenched market skepticism. The monarchy's structure reduces electoral fiscal loosening risk but concentrates adjustment tolerance in a small decision-making circle that has historically prioritized social stability over fiscal consolidation. The 2025–2026 reform package (CIT, energy pricing) represents a meaningful credibility signal, but sustained implementation over 2–3 years is required before creditor confidence can materially improve.

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Gross government debt/GDP rises above 140% without a credible IMF/GCC-endorsed consolidation path
- FX reserves fall below 1.5 months of total imports, materially raising vulnerability to a sudden stop
- Sustained oil price below $60/bbl for 12+ months, widening the fiscal deficit beyond 13% of GDP
- GCC bilateral financial support mechanism weakens, is conditioned, or is withdrawn, reducing the implicit backstop

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Primary surplus of at least 0.5% of GDP maintained for two consecutive years, demonstrating fiscal adjustment credibility
- Gross government debt/GDP placed on a credible declining trajectory below 130% over 24 months
- FX reserves strengthened above 3 months of total imports with explicit IMF endorsement
- CIT and subsidy reform revenues exceed 2% of GDP annually on a sustained basis, reducing structural dependence on hydrocarbon receipts

---

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Bahrain presents the most extreme debt/GDP ratio in the GCC — 134% in 2024 and rising — combined with a structural fiscal deficit persisting at 9–11% of GDP for six consecutive years, reserve coverage below IMF adequacy thresholds, and a high share of FX-denominated external debt requiring constant market access. The credit case rests on three pillars: an unblemished payment track record backed by GCC bilateral support unavailable to most B-rated sovereigns; a growing non-hydrocarbon sector providing partial insulation from oil price cycles; and nascent fiscal reform (CIT, subsidy rationalization) that, if sustained, could eventually inflect the debt trajectory. Moody's shift to Negative outlook on April 18, 2026 — three days before this note — aligns precisely with our assessment that the balance of risks is skewed to the downside absent accelerated consolidation. The 12–18 month horizon is critical: demonstrated fiscal improvement in the 2026–2027 budget cycle could support an outlook stabilization; failure to consolidate raises the probability of a B3 downgrade.

> **We assign the Kingdom of Bahrain an implied rating of B2 with a Negative Outlook, reflecting an extraordinarily elevated debt burden of 134% of GDP with no near-term stabilization path under current policies, partially offset by the GCC bilateral support backstop and an unblemished payment track record that differentiates Bahrain from typical B-rated sovereigns.**
