| **Prior Rating** | Moody's: Baa2/Stable; Fitch: BBB/Stable; S&P: BBB+/Positive Outlook |
| **Action** | **Affirm Baa2 / Stable Outlook** |

> **Rationale:** The Philippines maintains investment-grade status supported by robust GDP growth (~5.6% in 2026), a resilient remittance-driven current account, strong FX reserves ($112.5bn, 7.4 months of imports), and a well-managed banking system. S&P's Positive Outlook (with BBB+ rating) signals the potential for an upgrade within 24 months if fiscal metrics improve. The primary credit constraint is a fiscal deficit of approximately 5.3% of GDP in 2026 that is driving debt/GDP toward a 20-year high of ~65% — above the IMF's 60% benchmark — and the current account deficit (-3% GDP) at the manageable threshold. With divergence between Moody's (Baa2) and S&P (BBB+), we anchor the implied rating at Baa2 with Stable Outlook, with positive momentum contingent on fiscal consolidation.

---

## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **Robust GDP growth:** IMF projects **5.6% growth in 2026**, driven by remittance-fueled domestic consumption, resilient services exports (BPO/IT), and infrastructure investment under the Build Better More program; the Philippines has been one of Asia's most consistent growth performers in the post-COVID period.
- **Strong reserve position:** Gross international reserves reached **US$112.5bn** (January 2026), covering **7.4 months of imports** — well above the international 3–4 month standard; gold holdings at a record $20.7bn (January 2026) add further resilience; reserves up 9% year-on-year.
- **Remittance-backed external buffer:** Overseas Filipino Worker (OFW) remittances of approximately US$38–40bn annually provide a structural current account offset, limiting the external financing gap; this inflow source is counter-cyclical and diversified across dozens of host countries.
- **S&P Positive Outlook signal:** S&P's Positive Outlook on BBB+ (above Moody's Baa2) reflects a credible upgrade pathway over 24 months if fiscal and current account deficits narrow as projected; S&P also sees room for further upgrade through 2028.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Deteriorating fiscal position:** The 2026 budget targets a deficit of **5.3% of GDP** and debt-to-GDP is projected to reach **65.7%** — a 20-year high — as pandemic-era borrowing has not been fully unwound; the government's own target of returning to 60% debt/GDP by 2030 requires sustained consolidation.
- **Primary balance in deficit:** With a 5.3% overall deficit and interest payments estimated at ~2.5–3% of GDP, the primary balance is in deficit, meaning regular revenues do not cover non-interest expenditures — an adverse DSA signal for debt trajectory.
- **Current account deficit at threshold:** At **-3.2% of GDP (2025)** and projected **-3.0% (2026)**, the current account is at the DSA manageable threshold; trade deficit driven by capital goods imports for infrastructure is partially mitigated by remittances, but the overall BOP is shifting to deficit in 2025–2026.
- **South China Sea geopolitical tensions:** Escalating confrontations with China in the South China Sea — particularly around the Second Thomas Shoal — create persistent geopolitical risk, with potential to disrupt investment confidence, regional trade, and tourism inflows if tensions escalate beyond current levels.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Philippines' debt/GDP is projected to peak at approximately 65.7% in 2026, driven by fiscal deficits of 5–5.3% of GDP and the PHP depreciation effect on FX-denominated external obligations. The government's medium-term consolidation path targets a return to 60% debt/GDP by 2030, requiring gradual deficit reduction toward ~3% of GDP. The r-g differential is modestly unfavorable: with real interest rates on government debt (~2.5–3%) and real growth of ~5.5%, the growth differential is actually favorable — GDP growth should help reduce the debt ratio over time if the primary balance is brought to equilibrium. The critical path is achieving a primary surplus of at least 0.5–1% of GDP within the next 2–3 years, which would put debt firmly on a declining trajectory.

### 3C. External Liquidity

The Philippines' external liquidity is robust and improving. At US$112.5bn in reserves covering 7.4 months of imports, the country comfortably exceeds all adequacy metrics. The current account deficit of -3% GDP generates a gross external financing need of approximately US$15bn annually, but this is more than offset by FDI inflows (~US$5–8bn) and the large OFW remittance flow (~US$38–40bn). The BOP is shifting to deficit in 2025–2026 driven by portfolio outflows, but the underlying current account and remittance structure provides a durable external support base.

**Synthesis:** Philippines' willingness to pay is not in question. The sovereign's track record, institutional quality, and reserves comfortably support debt service continuity. The key medium-term risk is fiscal trajectory — whether the Marcos administration's medium-term consolidation plan is sustained given the 2025 midterm elections and the traditional pattern of pre-election spending acceleration in Philippine politics.

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Fiscal deficit fails to narrow from 5.3% of GDP, pushing debt/GDP above 70% within 3 years
- South China Sea military escalation disrupts FDI, tourism, and remittance confidence
- Typhoon season of unusual severity requires deficit spending >2% GDP, derailing the consolidation path
- BSP independence compromised by political pressure, triggering inflation overshoot

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Primary balance returns to surplus (>0.5% GDP) for two consecutive years, bending the debt trajectory
- S&P upgrades to A- (would be the highest-ever rating for the Philippines)
- South China Sea tensions diplomatically de-escalated, reducing the geopolitical risk premium
- Remittances exceed $42bn annually, providing additional current account cushion

---

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

The Philippines represents a high-quality investment-grade credit with one of Asia's most consistent growth records, supported by a structural remittance inflow, a credible central bank, and improving reserve coverage. The primary credit risk is fiscal: pandemic-era borrowing has pushed debt/GDP toward a 20-year high (~65%), and the 5.3% fiscal deficit target for 2026 is insufficient for near-term stabilization. The r-g differential is structurally favorable (5.5% real growth vs. 2.5–3% real interest costs) — if the government maintains fiscal discipline, the debt ratio will naturally decline toward the 60% target by 2030. The S&P Positive Outlook at BBB+ provides a bullish signal on the upgrade trajectory; the divergence between Moody's and S&P reflects different weights on the fiscal deterioration vs. growth resilience trade-off. The Philippines is appropriately rated at Baa2/BBB, with the upgrade case becoming executable once the primary balance turns positive.

> **We assign the Philippines an implied rating of Baa2 with a Stable Outlook, reflecting robust 5.6% GDP growth, US$112.5bn in reserves, and a strong remittance base, constrained by a fiscal deficit of 5.3% of GDP pushing debt toward a 20-year high of 65%, and a current account deficit at the manageable threshold.**
