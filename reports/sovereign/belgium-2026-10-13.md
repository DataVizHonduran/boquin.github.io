Belgium was downgraded by both Moody's (to A1 from Aa3, 2026-04-17) and S&P (to AA- from AA, 2026-04-24) within the same week, each citing the government's inability to implement measures sufficient to stabilize the rising debt burden despite politically ambitious fiscal and structural reforms enacted in 2025 [[sources below]]. The IMF's 2026 Article IV (concluded 2026-02-09) frames the economy as "resilient to successive shocks but scarred through large structural fiscal deficits, a rising public debt, deteriorated price-competitiveness, and a weaker external position" — a multi-dimensional deterioration rather than a single-metric problem.

## §2 Key Rating Drivers

**Strengths**

- **Structural external and macroeconomic strengths.** Both Moody's and S&P explicitly balanced their downgrade rationale against Belgium's underlying "structural external and macroeconomic strengths" — a diversified, open economy with deep institutional capacity, even as near-term fiscal metrics deteriorate.
- **High-value-added, diversified economy.** Belgium's position as a European logistics, EU-institutional, and pharmaceutical/chemicals hub provides structural resilience that is not fully captured in headline fiscal ratios.
- **Deep market access despite deterioration.** Belgium continues to place substantial government bond volumes at manageable (if rising) yields, reflecting enduring investor confidence in the broader institutional and EU-anchoring framework.

**Constraints**

- **Rising debt with no near-term stabilization.** The IMF projects debt/GDP reaching 107.2% in 2025 (among the highest in the eurozone) and rising steadily to 121.4% by 2031; the European Commission's Debt Sustainability Monitor flags Belgium alongside France, Italy and Finland for persistently high gross financing needs.
- **Wide and stable-at-elevated fiscal deficit.** The general government deficit widened to an estimated 5.2% of GDP in 2025 from 4.4% in 2024, driven by higher defense spending (+0.5pp) and age-related social benefits (+0.4pp); the IMF projects the deficit stabilizing above 5% of GDP through 2029 before rising again to 5.6% by 2031 as a planned tax cut takes effect.
- **Deteriorated price competitiveness and weaker external position.** The IMF explicitly cites deteriorated price-competitiveness and a weaker external balance as structural scars alongside the fiscal deterioration, with the current account running a deficit of roughly -2.3% to -2.6% of GDP in 2025-2026 (European Commission).

## §3B Debt Trajectory

Belgium's debt trajectory is among the most concerning in the eurozone, with no credible near-term stabilization path under current policy. The IMF's 2026 Article IV projects debt/GDP rising from 107.2% in 2025 to 121.4% by 2031, driven by a deficit that "broadly stabilizes above 5 percent of GDP through 2029, before rising again to reach 5.6 percent by 2031" as a previously-planned tax cut takes effect — meaning the fiscal trajectory worsens rather than improves even under the government's own medium-term plan. The April 2026 downgrades from Moody's and S&P both cited this specific failure: the government's inability to implement measures sufficient to stabilize the debt burden despite "politically ambitious fiscal and structural reforms enacted in 2025," a notable acknowledgment that effort has been made without yet producing results.

The IMF identifies three structural, demographically- and geopolitically-driven pressures compounding the arithmetic: an aging population raising age-related social benefit costs (+0.4pp of GDP in the 2025 deterioration alone), green transition costs, and increased defense spending (+0.5pp of GDP in 2025) — all pressures expected to persist or intensify through the medium term. The European Commission's 2025 Debt Sustainability Monitor groups Belgium with France, Italy and Finland among eurozone sovereigns with persistently high gross financing needs, driven by maturing debt and ongoing deficits, meaning rollover volumes will remain large even as yields rise (10Y Belgian yield forecast climbing toward 4.3% by Q4-2026, Trading Economics).

## §3C External Liquidity

Belgium's external position has weakened alongside its fiscal position, a combination the IMF explicitly flags as a structural "scar." The current account ran a deficit of roughly -2.3% of GDP in 2025, forecast to widen slightly to -2.6% in 2026 (European Commission) — not yet breaching the -3% DSA benchmark but moving in the wrong direction and reflecting the IMF-cited deterioration in price-competitiveness. As a euro-area member, Belgium has no independent reserve-adequacy constraint in the EM sense; FX backstop functions sit with the Eurosystem.

Gross external debt reached 242.8% of GDP in 2025 (Trading Economics/CEIC), a whole-economy figure reflecting Belgium's financial-sector and EU-institutional hub role rather than sovereign external borrowing per se, almost entirely euro-denominated. The combination of a widening (if still modest) current account deficit and a weakening competitiveness position is a genuine, IMF-flagged vulnerability trend, even though sudden-stop risk of the classical EM type remains low given euro-area membership and continued deep market access.

## §8 Rating Sensitivities

**Downgrade triggers**
- Debt/GDP trajectory exceeding the IMF's 121.4%-by-2031 baseline without new offsetting measures
- The planned 2031 tax cut proceeding without credible offsetting consolidation
- Current account deficit widening beyond the -3% of GDP DSA benchmark on a sustained basis

**Upgrade triggers**
- Deeper structural reforms beyond the 2025 package that credibly stabilize or reverse the debt trajectory
- Defense and age-related spending pressures contained below current IMF projections through further pension/structural reform
- Price-competitiveness and current account position stabilizing or improving from current levels

## §9 Analyst Conclusion

Belgium's credit profile has deteriorated on a genuinely broad front — fiscal, debt, competitiveness, and external balance simultaneously — rather than through a single shock, prompting a two-agency downgrade in April 2026 that explicitly cited the government's failure to stabilize debt despite real reform effort in 2025. The IMF's own medium-term baseline shows no stabilization before 2031, with structural pressures from aging, green transition, and defense spending all pushing in the same direction; the key open question is whether Belgium's "politically ambitious" reform agenda can be deepened, or whether coalition-governance constraints will continue to limit the pace of consolidation.

> **We assign Belgium an implied rating of AA-/A1 with a Stable Outlook, reflecting a genuine, multi-dimensional fiscal and competitiveness deterioration only partially offset by enacted 2025 reforms, with no credible debt-stabilization path visible before 2031 under current policy.**
