| **Action** | **Affirm Ba1 / Maintain Stable Outlook** |

> **Rationale:** Brazil's sovereign credit profile is defined by a fundamental tension: one of the world's largest and most diversified emerging economies — with extraordinary commodity wealth, a US$360bn reserve buffer, and deep domestic capital markets — burdened by a public debt ratio (~89% of GDP) that is among the highest in the BB cohort and an interest burden that consumes approximately 6–7% of GDP annually. Moody's Outlook cut from Positive to Stable in May 2025 reflects the failure of the new fiscal framework to deliver sufficient primary surplus consolidation, with the Selic rate at 15% amplifying debt dynamics in an adverse r-g feedback loop. The 2026 presidential election cycle introduces pre-election spending risk that further constrains the upgrade pathway. The credit is appropriately anchored at Ba1 with a Stable Outlook — strong enough to avoid near-term default risk, not strong enough to return to investment grade without a structural fiscal adjustment.

---

## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **Exceptional reserve position:** Gross FX reserves of **US$360bn** (November 2025), covering approximately **16 months of imports** — among the highest reserve coverage ratios globally — provide an extraordinary external liquidity buffer that essentially eliminates near-term external financing risk.
- **Commodity export resilience:** Brazil's status as the world's leading exporter of soybeans, coffee, iron ore, and beef, with growing oil production (pre-salt fields), provides a structurally robust current account export base; diversified commodity exposure reduces single-commodity price volatility.
- **Credible central bank:** The Banco Central do Brasil (BCB) has maintained operational independence, driving inflation to 4.26% in 2025 (within the 3%±1.5% continuous target band) despite fiscal pressures; with the Selic at 15%, real rates of ~10.7% demonstrate genuine monetary orthodoxy.
- **Deep domestic capital markets:** Brazil's domestic bond market (LTN, NTN-F, NTN-B) absorbs the vast majority of government financing domestically in BRL; FX-denominated external debt is only ~10–15% of total public debt, minimizing rollover risk from external financing dependency.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Unsustainably high public debt:** At approximately **89% of GDP** (gross general government), Brazil's debt ratio is the highest in the BB cohort; with interest payments at ~6–7% of GDP (~17% of revenue) and real interest rates at 10%+, the debt is self-reinforcing absent a sustained primary surplus well above 1% of GDP.
- **Fiscal framework credibility under question:** The 2026 budget targets a primary surplus of only **0.25% of GDP** — insufficient to stabilize the debt/GDP ratio given the interest burden; the fiscal rule has already been amended multiple times under the Lula administration, and analysts have flagged "paper surpluses" that exclude large expenditures from the primary balance calculation; Moody's May 2025 Outlook cut directly cited this credibility erosion.
- **Pre-election fiscal risk:** With presidential elections in October 2026, historical patterns of electoral fiscal loosening — social spending acceleration, minimum wage adjustments, transfers — create material risk of primary balance undershooting; Fitch explicitly cited the 2026 election as a factor delaying fiscal resolution until 2027.
- **Subdued growth:** IMF projects only **1.6% GDP growth in 2026** (World Bank: 2.2%), reflecting the contractionary impact of the 15% Selic rate on domestic demand; weak growth reduces the revenue denominator effect on debt/GDP and makes fiscal consolidation structurally harder.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Brazil's debt trajectory is the central DSA challenge. With gross general government debt at ~89% of GDP and projected to rise toward 95% by 2026 under IMF assumptions, the debt path is unsustainable without structural fiscal adjustment. The r-g differential is sharply adverse: real interest rates on government debt (~10%+) far exceed real GDP growth (1–2%), meaning that the debt ratio is mechanically rising unless the primary surplus exceeds approximately 2.5–3% of GDP — a target the government has not yet credibly committed to. The fiscal rule's tolerance band of ±0.25% of the primary balance target effectively permits near-zero adjustment, insufficient to bend the debt curve. Under the IMF baseline, debt/GDP reaches ~95% by 2026–2027 before potentially stabilizing if the 2027 successor government implements more aggressive consolidation.

### 3C. External Liquidity

Brazil's external liquidity position is paradoxically its strongest credit attribute. US$360bn in gross reserves representing 16 months of import coverage virtually eliminates near-term external financing risk. Brazil's current account deficit of -3% GDP is fully financed by FDI inflows (~3–4% GDP annually), meaning the overall balance of payments is not structurally stressed. The BRL depreciation in late 2024–early 2025 reflected fiscal credibility concerns rather than balance of payments stress; the currency has partially recovered and remains a floating rate that absorbs external shocks. External sovereign debt is minimal (~10–15% of total), so international capital markets access, while important for sovereign image, is not critical for near-term funding.

| Governance & Rule of Law (WGI) | **3.5** | WGI scores in the 50th–60th percentile; strong rule of law for a large EM; independent judiciary and electoral institutions; Lula's third term has not materially eroded institutional quality |
| Fiscal Policy Credibility | **2.5** | The new fiscal framework is credible in design but has been repeatedly amended to exclude large spending items; Moody's May 2025 Outlook cut and widespread analyst skepticism about "paper surpluses" reflect genuine credibility erosion; 2026 election risk exacerbates |
| Central Bank Independence | **4.5** | BCB has demonstrated genuine independence under new central bank law; maintained Selic at 15% despite presidential public pressure for cuts; institutional independence is now legally entrenched; strong anti-inflation credibility |
| Debt Payment Track Record | **4.5** | Brazil has maintained continuous sovereign debt service; no defaults since the 1980s debt crisis; the 2002 near-crisis was managed without default; strong debt management by STN/Tesouro Nacional |

**Synthesis:** Brazil's willingness to pay is not in question — the sovereign's institutional capacity, reserve position, and track record fully support debt service continuity. The credit challenge is structural fiscal arithmetic: with interest payments at 6–7% of GDP, the government's ability to generate the primary surpluses needed to stabilize debt/GDP depends on political choices that the Lula administration has been unwilling to make at the required scale. The 2026 election complicates the fiscal timeline, but investors expect fiscal reckoning to begin in earnest post-2026.

Brazil's credit profile within the BB/Ba cohort is marked by the contrast between its extraordinary external liquidity (16 months reserves — the best in the peer set by far) and its deeply adverse fiscal position (89% debt/GDP — the worst). This structural dichotomy means Brazil trades wider than its reserve position alone would suggest. The path to investment grade — last held in 2015 — requires the kind of entitlement reform that successive Brazilian governments have been unable or unwilling to deliver, making it a medium-term aspiration rather than a near-term prospect.

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Primary balance returns to deficit in 2026 due to electoral spending acceleration, with debt/GDP rising above 95% without a credible stabilization plan
- BRL depreciation exceeds 20% sustained, reigniting inflation above 7–8% and forcing Selic back to 17%+ — compounding the interest cost spiral
- Fiscal rule amendment removing the primary surplus requirement, signaling permanent abandonment of fiscal discipline
- Banking sector stress triggered by high interest rates and corporate debt restructurings requiring sovereign recapitalization

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Post-2026 election government implements structural spending cap or entitlement reform, credibly delivering primary surplus of 1.5%+ GDP for two consecutive years
- Debt/GDP ratio peaks and begins declining durably below 85% within a 3-year forecast horizon
- Selic rate declines to 9–10% without inflation reacceleration, reducing the interest burden and improving the growth-rate differential
- Investment-grade rating reinstated by one major agency (most likely Moody's given Ba1 starting point)

---

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Brazil's sovereign credit profile is defined by a structural paradox: the most externally resilient large emerging market (US$360bn reserves, low FX external debt, commodity export dominance) burdened by the most challenging domestic fiscal dynamic in the BB cohort (89% debt/GDP, 6–7% GDP interest burden, r-g differential of ~8 percentage points). The Moody's May 2025 Positive-to-Stable Outlook revision reflects the reality that the new fiscal framework, while institutionally sound in design, has not delivered the primary adjustment needed to bend the debt trajectory — and with presidential elections in October 2026, the consolidation window is effectively closed until 2027. BCB's maintenance of the Selic at 15% despite political pressure demonstrates that monetary credibility is intact; the challenge is entirely fiscal and political. Brazil will likely remain at Ba1/BB for the foreseeable future absent a post-election structural fiscal commitment, with meaningful upgrade potential emerging only if the 2026 election produces a mandate for entitlement reform that has eluded Brazil for three decades.

> **We assign Brazil an implied rating of Ba1 with a Stable Outlook, reflecting extraordinary reserve buffers and commodity export strength offset by an unsustainable 89% debt/GDP ratio, an adverse r-g differential driven by 15% interest rates, and fiscal credibility erosion under the Lula administration's resistance to structural adjustment.**
