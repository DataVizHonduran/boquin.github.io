| **Action** | **Affirm Aa2 / Stable Outlook** |

> **Rationale:** The UAE is among the highest-rated sovereigns in the EMBI universe, reflecting an extraordinary combination of sovereign wealth assets (ADIA + Mubadala estimated at US$1+ trillion), a persistent current account surplus (~7.9% GDP in 2025), low public debt (~28–32% GDP), and one of the most advanced non-oil diversification profiles in the GCC (non-oil GDP ~80% of total output). With all three major rating agencies at AA/Aa2 and Stable Outlooks, the credit case is unambiguous. Near-term downside risks — oil price volatility, geopolitical exposure, and federal-emirate fiscal fragmentation — are substantially mitigated by the sovereign's extraordinary balance sheet. The Aa2 rating is appropriate and likely to be maintained over the 3–5 year horizon.

---

## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **Extraordinary sovereign wealth position:** Abu Dhabi's sovereign wealth assets (ADIA, Mubadala, ADQ) total an estimated US$1+ trillion, representing one of the highest sovereign net asset positions globally; these assets provide an almost unlimited fiscal backstop and underpin the sovereign's AA-tier creditworthiness independently of current fiscal flows.
- **Advanced non-oil diversification:** Non-oil sectors account for approximately **80% of UAE GDP** — the highest in the GCC — reflecting decades of investment in financial services (DIFC), logistics, tourism, real estate, and technology; the IMF specifically applauded this diversification in its 2025 assessment.
- **Persistent current account surplus:** The UAE maintains a current account surplus of **~7.9% of GDP in 2025**, narrowing to ~6.5% in 2026 as oil prices stabilize; this external surplussus reflects both hydrocarbon export strength and the UAE's income from financial services, tourism, and investment returns.
- **Robust growth:** IMF projects **5.0% GDP growth in 2026**, driven by non-oil sector momentum, tourism recovery, real estate, and OPEC+ production normalization; Dubai's infrastructure and Expo 2020 legacy continue to generate above-peer growth rates.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Structural oil dependency:** Despite non-oil diversification, hydrocarbon revenues remain critical to Abu Dhabi's fiscal position; the consolidated fiscal surplus (~5.5% GDP, 2025) is vulnerable to oil price shocks, and the federal fiscal framework partially shields but does not eliminate this exposure.
- **Geopolitical risk:** The UAE's proximity to Iran, Yemen, and active regional conflicts imposes a permanent geopolitical risk premium; the 2022 Houthi drone strikes on Abu Dhabi were a sharp reminder of infrastructure vulnerability, and the Gulf's security environment remains fragile.
- **Federal-emirate fiscal fragmentation:** The UAE's federal structure means that fiscal data is complex — Abu Dhabi's ADIA/Mubadala assets are not consolidated with the federal government; transparency on the full extent of sovereign wealth and contingent liabilities is limited relative to single-sovereign peers at the same rating level.
- **Real estate concentration risk:** The UAE (particularly Dubai) has experienced real estate boom-bust cycles; rapid residential price appreciation in 2022–2025 introduces mortgage and banking sector risk if the cycle reverses, creating potential contingent fiscal liabilities.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

UAE public debt is on a declining trajectory, having fallen 3.9% year-on-year in 2025 to AED 112.4bn, representing approximately 20.8% of GDP (federal measure) to 32% (general government basis). With fiscal surpluses of approximately 5–5.5% of GDP in recent years — driven by Abu Dhabi's hydrocarbon revenues and growing non-oil fiscal receipts — the debt trajectory is structurally favorable. Even under an oil price shock scenario (Brent at US$60/barrel), the sovereign wealth fund backstop would prevent any acute funding pressure for at least a decade without structural adjustment. The r-g differential is strongly negative (favorable), with nominal GDP growth outpacing interest costs by a wide margin.

### 3C. External Liquidity

The UAE's external liquidity is among the strongest globally. CBUAE official reserves, ADIA, Mubadala, ADQ, and Abu Dhabi's fiscal stabilization funds provide a multi-layered liquidity structure that no plausible external shock could deplete. The AED currency peg to the USD (in place since 1971) is credibly maintained by this reserve structure and is not subject to speculative pressure given the sovereign's balance sheet. External debt obligations are modest relative to sovereign asset stocks, and the current account surplus means the UAE is a net capital exporter rather than a recipient of external financing.

## 4. WILLINGNESS TO PAY

| Dimension | Score (1–5) | Commentary |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Governance & Rule of Law (WGI) | **3.5** | WGI scores in the 60th–70th percentile, reflecting effective governance by regional standards; commercial law and DIFC's common law courts provide a strong business environment; political concentration under the ruling families of Abu Dhabi and Dubai is a structural feature |
| Fiscal Policy Credibility | **5.0** | The UAE has maintained consistent fiscal surpluses, building sovereign wealth over decades; debt management is transparent and professionally managed via the Public Debt Management Office; no history of fiscal populism |
| Central Bank Independence | **3.5** | CBUAE manages the USD peg with professional competence; independence is structurally constrained by the fixed exchange rate framework but the peg is credibly maintained; inflation is low (~3–4%) |
| Debt Payment Track Record | **5.0** | Uninterrupted sovereign debt service; Dubai 2009 debt restructuring (Dubai World, not sovereign) was the only material credit event, and was resolved without sovereign default; Abu Dhabi provided the backstop then and would do so again |

**Synthesis:** UAE willingness to pay is beyond question — the sovereign has the financial capacity to service its entire outstanding debt stock multiple times over from sovereign wealth assets alone. The primary credit risks are structural (oil price, geopolitics, real estate) rather than payment-capacity related. The Dubai World 2009 episode demonstrated that Abu Dhabi will backstop systemic risk, reinforcing the implicit sovereign guarantee framework.

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Sustained oil prices below US$50/barrel for 2+ years, shifting the consolidated fiscal position to a deficit and requiring large-scale sovereign wealth drawdowns
- Major geopolitical escalation (Iran-Gulf conflict) that disrupts trade, tourism, or oil infrastructure materially
- Real estate sector correction of >25% triggering banking sector stress requiring sovereign recapitalization
- Loss of AED peg credibility due to extended reserve drawdowns, though this scenario is extremely remote

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Non-oil fiscal revenues reach self-sufficiency (covering non-defense expenditures without hydrocarbon revenues), eliminating oil price vulnerability
- Governance improvements: enhanced fiscal transparency, independent institutions, or democratic accountability mechanisms
- Sovereign wealth assets continued to grow, further widening the gap between net asset position and gross obligations
- AA/Aa1 equivalency achieved across all three rating agencies simultaneously

---

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

The UAE represents one of the strongest sovereign credit profiles accessible to investors through the EMBI universe — an AA-rated sovereign with extraordinary sovereign wealth, a persistent current account surplus, and an advanced non-oil diversification trajectory that has already achieved 80% non-oil GDP contribution. The credit risks are real but well-contained: oil price sensitivity is mitigated by ADIA's trillion-dollar buffer; geopolitical risk is a permanent structural feature at this rating level; real estate cycle risk is backstopped by Abu Dhabi's sovereign capacity. The three major agencies are aligned at AA/Aa2 with Stable Outlooks, and this alignment is likely to persist. The UAE offers investors the rare combination of high-grade credit quality with an EM yield premium that increasingly reflects structural rather than cyclical risk. The primary credit debate at this level is not default risk — which is essentially zero over any foreseeable time horizon — but rather the pace of diversification and whether the non-oil revenue base can sustain sovereign quality beyond the hydrocarbon era.

> **We assign the UAE an implied rating of Aa2 with a Stable Outlook, reflecting extraordinary sovereign wealth assets exceeding US$1 trillion, advanced non-oil diversification, persistent current account surplus, and low debt, offset by oil price vulnerability, geopolitical risk, and governance concentration in the ruling families.**
