## §1 RATING ACTION & OUTLOOK

| Sovereign | Date | Implied Rating | Outlook | Moody's | Fitch | S&P | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| South Africa | 2026-05-14 | BB- | Stable | Ba2 (Stable) | BB- (Stable) | BB / BB+ LC (Positive) | Affirm — Debt trajectory still upward despite S&P upgrade momentum |

South Africa's implied BB- rating with Stable outlook reflects a balance between nascent reform momentum and entrenched fiscal vulnerabilities. S&P's landmark upgrade to BB (foreign currency) in 2025 — the first by a major agency in over 16 years — signals recognition of improvements in electricity supply, logistics reforms, and a narrowing current account deficit that reached near-balance (-0.5% of GDP) in 2025. However, gross government debt reached 77% of GDP at end-March 2025 and is projected to rise toward 84% by FY2031 under IMF baseline assumptions, maintaining a moderate risk of debt distress classification. The IMF's February 2026 Article IV concluded that "public debt has risen further" and that fiscal consolidation efforts, while credible in design, remain vulnerable to implementation risk and external shocks.

The Stable outlook is predicated on the government's ability to deliver on the November 2025 MTBPS fiscal consolidation path — targeting a primary surplus of 0.9% of GDP in FY2025/26 rising to 2.3% by FY2028 — while sustaining structural reforms in electricity and logistics. A Positive outlook revision would require evidence that debt/GDP has crested and is durably declining. A Negative bias would materialize on fiscal slippage, SOE contingent liability crystallization, or a growth shock sufficient to reverse the primary balance trajectory.

## §2 KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Strength 1: Credible Monetary Policy Anchor**
The South African Reserve Bank (SARB) demonstrated institutional credibility by successfully navigating a pivot to a 3% point inflation target, replacing the previous 3-6% band effective 2025. Headline CPI averaged 3.2% in 2025 (IMF Art IV, Feb 2026), falling within the new target corridor. The MPC held the repo rate at 6.75% in January and March 2026 (SARB MPC Statements, Jan-Mar 2026), with the quarterly projection model forecasting a gradual easing to 6.31% by end-2026. Central bank independence remains intact and is a key credit differentiator relative to regional peers.

**Strength 2: Current Account Improvement and Near-Balance External Position**
The current account deficit narrowed sharply to -0.5% of GDP for full-year 2025 (from -0.7% in 2024), with Q4 2025 delivering the first current account surplus since Q3 2023 (ZAR 50.2bn surplus, driven by a trade surplus of ZAR 282.2bn — SARB/Trading Economics, 2026). FX reserves stood at USD 48.4bn in October 2025, equivalent to 4.7 months of merchandise import cover (CEIC Data, 2025), above the IMF's 3-month adequacy threshold. This external resilience meaningfully limits sudden-stop vulnerability over the near term.

**Strength 3: Structural Reform Momentum — Energy and Logistics**
Load-shedding days in 2025 declined materially from 2024 peak levels, following private sector IPP market entry and Eskom operational improvements. The IMF Article IV (Feb 2026) projects that "ongoing electricity and logistics reforms are expected to boost investment over the medium run, with growth projected to reach 1.8 percent by the end of the decade." Transnet port and rail reforms, while still early-stage, represent a tangible structural inflection that underpins the S&P upgrade rationale and establishes a platform for medium-term growth acceleration.

**Constraint 1: Rising Debt Trajectory — Moderate DSA Risk**
Government gross debt reached 77% of GDP at end-March 2025 and is projected to continue rising, approaching ~84% of GDP by FY2031 under baseline assumptions (IMF Art IV, Feb 2026). This trajectory is driven by adverse real interest-growth differentials (r > g), persistent overall fiscal deficits (4.7% of GDP in FY2025/26), and SOE contingent liabilities. The IMF DSA classifies the overall risk of debt distress as **moderate**, with limited fiscal space to absorb shocks.

**Constraint 2: Structurally Weak Growth — Well Below Potential**
GDP growth averaged 1.3% in 2025 and is projected at only 1.4% in 2026 and 1.5% in 2027 (IMF WEO April 2026). This compares with an estimated 3%+ growth threshold required to stabilize debt at current effective interest rates. Structural impediments — unemployment at ~33% (expanded definition ~42%), inadequate infrastructure, rigid labor markets, and governance deficits — constrain TFP accumulation and absorptive capacity. Without reforms translating into higher investment and productivity, the growth-interest differential will remain unfavorable for debt dynamics.

**Constraint 3: SOE Contingent Liabilities and Governance Deficits**
State-owned enterprises — principally Eskom and Transnet — carry large off-balance-sheet contingent liabilities that intermittently crystallize onto the sovereign balance sheet. South Africa scores below the global median on the World Bank WGI Control of Corruption dimension (~43rd percentile estimated, 2023 vintage), reflecting legacy governance deficiencies from the "state capture" era. While institutional recovery is underway — NPA prosecutions, new Public Procurement Act (2023) — the pace of governance improvement is uneven, and SOE reform execution risk remains a binding rating constraint.

## §3B DEBT TRAJECTORY

South Africa's gross government debt reached 77% of GDP at end-March 2025, up from approximately 40% in 2015, reflecting a decade of primary deficits, weak growth, and SOE bailout pressures (Eskom alone received transfers exceeding 2% of GDP in peak years). The IMF's February 2026 Article IV baseline projects further increases toward 84% of GDP by FY2031, absent a decisive improvement in the primary balance. The growth-adjusted interest burden is the dominant driver: with real GDP growth of 1.3-1.4% and effective real interest rates materially above this level, automatic debt dynamics remain adverse even as the primary balance moves into surplus. The government's fiscal strategy targets debt stabilization near current levels and a long-run reduction toward approximately 70% of GDP — an achievable but ambitious path requiring sustained execution.

The November 2025 MTBPS affirms a primary surplus of 0.9% of GDP in FY2025/26 — the first meaningful primary surplus after several years of deficit — rising to 1.7% in FY2026/27 and 2.5% by FY2028/29. The IMF Article IV endorses this path but requires the primary surplus to reach approximately 1.5% by FY2026 and 2.3% by FY2028 for debt to stabilize and gradually decline toward the long-run target. Achieving this requires continued strong revenue performance (VAT and CIT collection remained robust in 2025) alongside disciplined expenditure control, including limiting further unbudgeted SOE transfers. The Budget Review 2026 (National Treasury, February 2026) reaffirms the consolidation framework, which the IMF Executive Board "welcomed" at the February 9, 2026 Article IV conclusion.

The maturity profile carries moderate rollover risk. South Africa's domestic debt is predominantly rand-denominated, limiting direct currency mismatch at the sovereign level, though structural ZAR depreciation against the dollar introduces implicit fiscal costs over time. External debt reached USD 200.3bn in Q4 2025 (CEIC), rising from USD 193bn in Q3, and includes a meaningful share of USD- and EUR-denominated instruments. Gross financing needs are substantial — in excess of 15% of GDP annually — given the requirement to roll maturing bonds and fund the residual deficit. The Treasury's strong domestic institutional investor base (pension funds, long-only insurance) provides a buffer against sudden-stop risk in the domestic market, but elevated domestic bond yields (10Y at ~8.63% in May 2026; Trading Economics) reflect the market's residual risk premium on fiscal sustainability.

## §3C EXTERNAL LIQUIDITY

South Africa's external liquidity position improved meaningfully in 2025. Gross international reserves reached USD 48.4bn in October 2025 (CEIC), equivalent to approximately 4.7 months of merchandise import cover — comfortably above the IMF's minimum adequacy threshold of 3 months and broadly consistent with the IMF ARA metric for a country of this income level and exchange rate regime. The current account deficit narrowed to just -0.5% of GDP for full-year 2025, with Q4 2025 registering the first current account surplus since Q3 2023, driven by a widening trade surplus to ZAR 282.2bn on stronger commodity export revenues and weaker imports. This structural improvement in the trade balance reduces the external financing requirement and diminishes near-term sudden-stop vulnerability. Nonetheless, gross external debt rose to USD 200.3bn in Q4 2025, indicating ongoing external liability accumulation that must be monitored against reserve adequacy.

The debt service burden is manageable in the near term but warrants monitoring as global financing conditions evolve. South Africa's gross external financing requirement — incorporating the residual current account deficit plus amortization of medium- and long-term external debt — is estimated at approximately 10-12% of GDP, serviceable given current reserve levels but leaving the sovereign exposed to a sharp exchange rate shock or a sudden tightening in EM external financing conditions. The rand remains one of the most liquid EM currencies but is historically volatile: during risk-off episodes (2015-16, 2018, 2022), ZAR depreciated 15-20% in short windows, amplifying the real cost of external debt servicing and fiscal costs of imports. The SARB's reserve buffer, combined with the near-balanced current account, provides approximately 12-18 months of adequate liquidity runway under a moderate stress scenario — sufficient but not robust against a severe EM-wide shock.

South Africa's willingness-to-pay profile is anchored by an exemplary payment track record and a genuinely independent central bank — among the strongest in the rating peer group — partially offset by governance vulnerabilities and a multi-year pattern of fiscal outturns weaker than stated targets. The Government of National Unity (GNU, formed May 2024) introduces coalition complexity but has thus far demonstrated coherence on fiscal policy, with the ANC and DA aligned on the consolidation framework. The key downside risk is political fragility: GNU fracture would likely weaken fiscal discipline commitments and create negative rating momentum. Absent that scenario, the WTP assessment supports the Ba2/BB- rating cluster without meaningful upgrade pressure until debt/GDP demonstrates a credible declining path.

South Africa trades at a modest spread premium to Morocco, reflecting higher debt levels and materially weaker GDP growth, though its near-balanced current account and stronger institutional framework — particularly SARB independence and a clean payment history — justify some compression toward Moroccan levels over time. The key relative value differentiator over a 3-year horizon is reform delivery: Morocco's superior growth track record and upgrade trajectory make it the aspirational peer benchmark for South Africa, provided electricity and logistics gains translate into sustained investment expansion.

## §8 RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Downgrade Triggers:**
- Gross government debt/GDP exceeds 85% on a sustained basis without a credible stabilization plan affirmed within 24 months, signaling a loss of debt anchor
- Primary balance reverts to deficit (below 0% of GDP) driven by structural fiscal slippage, unbudgeted SOE transfers exceeding 1.5% of GDP, or GNU political fracture forcing expansionary policy concessions
- FX reserves fall below 3.5 months of import cover on a sustained basis, reflecting deterioration in external liquidity, significant capital outflows, or a large current account deterioration driven by commodity price collapse

**Upgrade Triggers:**
- Gross government debt/GDP demonstrates a clear, multi-year declining trajectory toward 70% of GDP, supported by primary surpluses sustained above 2% of GDP for at least two consecutive fiscal years
- Real GDP growth accelerates durably above 2.5% annually, underpinned by evidence that electricity and logistics reform dividends are translating into measurable private investment expansion and employment creation
- WGI governance scores — particularly Control of Corruption and Government Effectiveness — show statistically meaningful improvement over two consecutive annual WGI editions, signaling a structural governance inflection beyond cyclical prosecutorial activity

---

## §9 ANALYST CONCLUSION

South Africa stands at a genuine — if fragile — inflection point in its sovereign credit trajectory. The S&P upgrade to BB, the SARB's successful transition to a 3% inflation target, a near-balanced current account, and nascent structural reform progress in energy and logistics all represent meaningful credit improvements relative to the 2020-2023 nadir. However, the IMF's assessment of "moderate" debt distress risk, the projected rise in gross debt toward 84% of GDP by FY2031, structurally weak growth of only 1.3-1.4%, high Gini-linked social spending pressure, and ongoing SOE contingent liability exposure temper optimism. The Government of National Unity provides political cover for fiscal consolidation but introduces coalition execution risk that is difficult to price with precision. The verdict is an affirmed BB- with Stable Outlook — acknowledging demonstrated progress while reserving upgrade capacity for sustained delivery on debt stabilization and the structural growth agenda.

> **We assign South Africa an implied rating of BB- with a Stable Outlook, reflecting a credit profile poised between nascent reform gains — including the first S&P upgrade in 16 years, credible SARB policy, and a near-balanced external account — and structurally elevated fiscal vulnerabilities, with gross government debt at 77% of GDP and rising toward 84% by FY2031, constraining the path to a higher rating absent sustained primary surplus delivery above 2% of GDP and structural growth acceleration durably above 2.5%.**
