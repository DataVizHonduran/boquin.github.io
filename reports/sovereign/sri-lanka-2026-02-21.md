## 1. RATING ACTION & OUTLOOK

| Item | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **Sovereign** | Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka |
| **Report Date** | 2026-02-21 |
| **Implied Rating** | **Caa1** |
| **Outlook** | **Positive** |
| **Prior Rating** | Moody's: Caa1 (Stable); Fitch: CCC+ (upgraded from RD, Dec 2024) |
| **Action** | **Affirm Caa1 / Revise Outlook to Positive** |

> **Rationale:** Sri Lanka has executed one of the most complex sovereign debt restructurings in recent memory, returned to IMF program compliance across five consecutive reviews, and delivered primary surpluses ahead of schedule. Debt/GDP has declined precipitously from its 2022 peak of ~119%, reaching ~93% by September 2025. Nonetheless, absolute debt burdens remain highly elevated, reserve coverage is still thin (3.1 months imports), governance is structurally weak, and the post-cyclone shock requires vigilance. The Positive outlook reflects credible consolidation momentum and a reform-oriented administration with a parliamentary supermajority.

---

## 2. KEY RATING DRIVERS

**Credit Strengths:**
- **IMF EFF compliance on track:** Five consecutive review completions (most recently staff-level agreement on 5th review, October 2025), unlocking ~US$3bn in aggregate EFF financing and providing policy anchor; program compliance signals institutional credibility not present during the 2022 crisis.
- **Primary fiscal surplus achieved and sustained:** The government delivered a primary surplus of approximately **3.8% of GDP in 2025**, well above the IMF program target of 2.3% — a remarkable turnaround from the pre-crisis fiscal collapse; the 2026 budget targets 2.5% primary surplus.
- **Debt restructuring completed:** ISB and bilateral debt restructuring finalized by end-2024/early 2025, materially reducing debt service burden; foreign debt service stands at a manageable US$2.1bn in 2026 vs. the pre-restructuring cliff.
- **Current account recovery:** Sri Lanka returned to current account surplus (~**1.8% of GDP in 2023**, accelerating in 2025 with Q1 2025 recording a record quarterly surplus of US$938m), reflecting tourism recovery, remittance inflows, and compressed import demand.

**Credit Constraints:**
- **Debt/GDP remains very high:** At ~93% of GDP (September 2025), well above the IMF's 60% benchmark, with the medium-term DSA target of sub-95% by 2032 leaving a prolonged period of elevated vulnerability; the interest-to-revenue ratio remains structurally elevated.
- **Reserve adequacy is tenuous:** At 3.1 months of import coverage (December 2025) and US$6.7bn gross reserves (January 2026), coverage is above the 3-month floor but leaves limited buffer against external shocks; the IMF revised down reserve projections for 2025–2026 in late 2025.
- **Cyclone Ditwah disruption:** A post-cyclone emergency required the IMF to approve US$206m in emergency financial support (December 2025), highlighting continuing vulnerability to climate-related shocks; the event pressured near-term growth and fiscal metrics.
- **Governance and institutional capacity:** WGI indicators place Sri Lanka in the 30th–45th percentile across most governance dimensions; the NPP government's reform agenda — including constitutional reform — is proceeding slowly, with the 2026 budget allocating no funds for the constitutional reform process.
- **Growth deceleration expected:** IMF projects GDP growth of **3.1%** for 2026 (World Bank: 3.5%), decelerating from the 4.8% recorded in H1 2025, reflecting cyclone aftermath, restrained public investment, and still-weak private sector confidence.

### 3B. Debt Trajectory Narrative

Under the IMF EFF baseline, public debt/GDP is projected to continue declining from ~93% (September 2025) toward the program target of sub-95% by 2032, conditional on sustained primary surpluses in the 2–3% of GDP range and real GDP growth averaging 3–4%. The r-g differential (real interest rate minus real growth rate) is currently near neutral: restructured debt carries concessional terms from bilateral creditors and below-market IMF rates, keeping the average effective interest rate below the nominal growth rate. However, as Sri Lanka returns to market borrowing and domestic interest rates normalize, the differential could turn adverse, making fiscal discipline non-negotiable for debt stabilization. A sustained primary surplus of at least **1.5% of GDP** appears necessary on a permanent basis to put debt on a firmly declining path.

### 3C. External Liquidity

With US$6.7bn in gross reserves (January 2026) covering 3.1 months of imports, Sri Lanka sits just above the conventional adequacy threshold but retains limited cushion against sudden stops or capital flow reversals. Foreign debt service of US$2.1bn in 2026 remains manageable relative to reserves, but the maturity profile of restructured ISBs — with principal payments concentrated in 2028–2033 — creates rollover risk that will require sustained reserve accumulation and successful re-engagement with international capital markets before the window opens.

## 4. WILLINGNESS TO PAY

| Dimension | Score (1–5) | Commentary |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Governance & Rule of Law (WGI) | **2.5** | WGI estimates place Sri Lanka in 30th–45th percentile on government effectiveness and rule of law; Freedom House rates the country "Partly Free" (2025). Improvement from 2022 crisis nadir. |
| Fiscal Policy Credibility | **3.5** | Primary surpluses consistently ahead of IMF targets since 2024 are credibility-building; however, the 2026 budget widens the overall deficit to 5.1% of GDP and reduces the primary surplus target, indicating some relaxation as the electoral cycle progresses. |
| Central Bank Independence | **3.0** | The Central Bank of Sri Lanka (CBSL) has maintained an orthodox monetary stance, guiding inflation to 2.1–2.3% (January 2026), well below the 5% target band; legislative reforms since 2023 have strengthened CBSL's operational independence. |
| Debt Payment Track Record | **2.0** | Sri Lanka defaulted on external obligations in April 2022 — the first default in its modern history — leaving a permanent mark on willingness-to-pay assessments. The orderly restructuring process and full IMF compliance partially rehabilitate this record, but re-entry into voluntary capital markets remains uncertain. |

**Synthesis:** The NPP government under President Anura Kumara Dissanayake, elected September 2024 with a parliamentary supermajority (159 of 225 seats), has demonstrated a pragmatic willingness to maintain IMF program continuity, reversing pre-election campaign rhetoric about renegotiating the EFF. This is the single most important willingness-to-pay signal post-default. However, structural governance challenges persist — anti-corruption prosecution has been limited, constitutional reform has stalled despite the political mandate, and the 2022 default remains a recent data point. We assess willingness to pay as **adequate but not yet established**.

## 7. ESG CONSIDERATIONS

| ESG Factor | Assessment |
|------------|------------|
| **E – Environmental** | Sri Lanka is highly exposed to climate risk — Cyclone Ditwah (2025) prompted emergency IMF financing and underscores contingent fiscal liability risk from natural disasters. The island's dependence on rain-fed agriculture and hydropower for electricity generation creates weather-related growth volatility. Climate vulnerability is a **negative credit factor** that increases the tail risk of fiscal shocks. |
| **S – Social** | The 2022 economic crisis inflicted severe social damage: poverty rates rose sharply, food insecurity affected millions, and emigration of skilled workers accelerated. Gini coefficient is moderate (~39) relative to region, but the political upheaval that ousted President Rajapaksa in 2022 reflects deep social fragility. The NPP's election on an anti-corruption, anti-inequality platform reflects unresolved social tensions. Social stability risk is **moderate and improving** but not resolved. |
| **G – Governance** | WGI scores place Sri Lanka in the 30th–45th percentile on most governance dimensions. The NPP administration has anti-corruption rhetoric but limited prosecutorial follow-through. Constitutional reform — including possible abolition of the executive presidency — is stalled. The 2022 default itself partly reflects governance failures in fiscal management over the prior decade. Press freedom and civil liberties are rated "Partly Free" (Freedom House 2025). Governance is a **persistent constraining factor** on the rating. |

## 8. RATING SENSITIVITIES

**Factors that could lead to a downgrade (negative pressure):**
- Debt/GDP stabilizes or rises above 100% on a sustained basis due to fiscal slippage or growth collapse
- IMF EFF program goes off-track, with missed performance criteria or unresolved waivers, signaling policy reversal
- Gross foreign reserves fall below 2 months of import coverage, triggering external liquidity stress
- Political instability — including reversal of the NPP administration's IMF commitment — leads to renewed debt service disruption
- A second major climate event or terms-of-trade shock exceeds the fiscal buffer capacity of the primary surplus

**Factors that could lead to an upgrade (positive pressure):**
- Primary surplus sustained above 2.5% of GDP for two or more consecutive years, with debt/GDP declining below 85%
- Successful voluntary market re-entry via international bond issuance at spread levels below 400 bps, demonstrating restored investor confidence
- IMF EFF successfully completed and replaced by a precautionary or surveillance arrangement, signaling graduation from crisis-era support
- Gross reserves rebuild to above 4 months of import coverage on a durable basis
- Meaningful governance improvements — including anti-corruption enforcement and independent judiciary strengthening — as captured by WGI percentile upgrades

---

## 9. ANALYST CONCLUSION

Sri Lanka has traversed the most acute phase of its 2022 sovereign debt crisis with greater policy discipline than most comparably-stressed sovereigns. The IMF EFF has served as both a financing lifeline and a credibility anchor, and the NPP government's decision to honor and extend the program — despite pre-election campaign skepticism — is the single most critical credit development since the default. Primary fiscal surpluses are running ahead of program targets, the current account has returned to surplus, and debt/GDP has declined by over 25 percentage points from its 2022 peak. These achievements are real and meaningful within the Caa rating bucket. Yet the credit profile remains fragile: absolute debt is still ~93% of GDP on a declining-but-extended path to medium-term sustainability, reserve buffers are thin, governance scores remain structurally weak, and the 2022 default remains a recent and formative event in the willingness-to-pay record. The post-cyclone emergency IMF disbursement of December 2025 serves as a reminder that Sri Lanka's recovery remains hostage to exogenous shocks that its still-limited fiscal space struggles to absorb independently. Upgrade pressure will build if fiscal consolidation persists and market access is re-established at manageable cost; downgrade risk re-emerges primarily through political reversal of the IMF program or a significant external shock.

> **We assign Sri Lanka an implied rating of Caa1 with a Positive Outlook, reflecting credible post-restructuring fiscal consolidation and IMF program adherence, constrained by still-elevated debt burdens, thin reserve buffers, and fragile governance underpinning the sovereign's long-run repayment capacity.**
