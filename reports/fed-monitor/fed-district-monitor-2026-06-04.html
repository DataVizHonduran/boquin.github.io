1. The Unintended Effects of Interest Rate Caps: Credit Reallocation to Safer Borrowers [NY]

This research highlights how state-level rate caps inadvertently push lenders toward low-risk borrowers to avoid losses. For our outlook, this suggests a tightening of credit availability for the "marginal" borrower, which could accelerate a slowdown in consumer spending among lower-income cohorts.

2. The Unintended Effects of Interest Rate Caps: Credit Rationing for Risky Borrowers [NY]

Complementing the previous study, this paper argues that rate caps lead to outright credit rationing rather than lower costs for the poor. This indicates that regulatory interventions intended to protect consumers may actually be creating "credit deserts," increasing the reliance on unregulated shadow banking.

3. Struggling Regional Small Businesses Deeply Pessimistic About 2026 Prospects [NY]

The updated indicators for the Second District show a sharp decline in sentiment among small businesses in NY, NJ, and CT. Given the systemic importance of this region, this pessimism serves as a leading indicator for a potential contraction in regional CAPEX and hiring through the remainder of the year.

4. Remote Work Leaves Younger Workers Sidelined [NY]

The analysis suggests a causal link between the rise of remote work and increased youth unemployment due to the loss of in-person mentorship and onboarding. This points to a long-term structural risk in human capital development that could dampen productivity growth and labor force participation for years to come.

Synthesis:

The data suggests a worrying convergence of credit contraction for risky borrowers and structural frictions in the youth labor market. We should brace for a scenario where regional small-business weakness and diminished labor productivity act as a drag on GDP growth despite broader monetary policy shifts.

Youth unemployment has risen dramatically since the pandemic—as has the prevalence of remote work. Our analysis suggests that these trends are related, with remote work making it more difficult for managers to train and mentor new employees. Accordingly, companies may be reluctant to hire less-experienced workers in distributed work arrangements.
