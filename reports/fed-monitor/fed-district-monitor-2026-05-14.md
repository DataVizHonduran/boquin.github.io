## 🔦 Today's Most Interesting Insights

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Investment Committee
**FROM:** Senior Economist
**DATE:** May 14, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Analysis of Recent Federal Reserve District Research

After reviewing the latest publications from the Federal Reserve districts, the available data is sparse, but two specific reports provide critical signals regarding systemic risk and growth catalysts.

1. **[NY] Federal Student Loan Defaults Return After Pandemic Pause**
The return of student loan defaults and a rise in total household debt to $18.8 trillion signal a tightening of the consumer credit cycle. This suggests that the "excess savings" cushion from the pandemic era has finally evaporated, increasing the risk of a consumption slowdown as debt service burdens rise.

2. **[NY] Will Mounting Supply Chain Strains Hamper the AI Investment Boom?**
The identification of a third global supply shock in six years—driven by Middle East instability—threatens the CAPEX cycle for AI infrastructure. If supply chain constraints limit the deployment of hardware, we may see a decoupling of AI software potential from actual productivity gains, creating a valuation risk for Big Tech.

3. **[RIC] Productivity Growth and Monetary Policy in the 1990s**
By revisiting the 1990s, the Richmond Fed is signaling a theoretical framework for how the FOMC should handle "productivity shocks." If AI mirrors the 90s boom, the Fed may be more inclined to tolerate higher growth rates without hiking rates, as productivity gains are less inflationary than demand-driven growth.

**Synthesis:** We are seeing a precarious tension between a fragile consumer balance sheet and a high-potential AI productivity cycle. The primary macro risk is that geopolitical supply shocks may stifle the very productivity gains needed to offset rising household debt and maintain the current growth trajectory.
