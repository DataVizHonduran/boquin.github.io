# Brazil DPF: Structural Shifts in Holder Composition

**Date**: March 29, 2026
**Source data**: Tesouro Nacional — Relatório Mensal da Dívida (RMD), Anexo 2.7
**Dashboard**: [boquin.xyz/reports/brasil-debt-holders/](https://boquin.xyz/reports/brasil-debt-holders/)

## Why Pension Fund Holdings Surged After 2016

Pension funds (Previdência category) increased their share of Brazil's Federal Public Debt
(DPF) markedly from ~2016, rising from roughly 16–17% of total DPF to over 22% by the
early 2020s.

### Four Contributing Factors

**1. Equity and Corporate Credit Collapse (~40%)**
The primary *source of flows*. Brazil's 2015–2016 recession — its worst in decades —
combined with the Lava Jato corruption investigations devastated domestic risk assets.
The Ibovespa fell ~40% from its 2013 peak; bonds from Petrobras, Eletrobras, OGX,
and utilities were impaired or defaulted. Large legacy funds (Previ, Funcef, Petros)
had hundreds of billions in AUM to reallocate. Government securities were the only
liquid, deep market that could absorb these flows.

**2. Selic and NTN-B Yields Easily Beat Actuarial Targets (~25%)**
The primary *destination pull*. Brazilian pension funds are required to earn INPC/IPCA
+ ~5–6% per year to meet their actuarial benchmarks. With the Selic at 14.25%
(July 2015–October 2016) and NTN-B (IPCA-linked Treasury) real yields well above
those targets, there was no incentive to take credit or equity risk. Government bonds
were risk-free and outperforming the benchmark. This factor explains *why* DPF
absorbed the rotation, not why pensions sold risk assets — so it is analytically
distinct from factor 1 but operationally inseparable.

**3. PREVIC Regulatory Pressure (~25%)**
An independent, additive force. Many large funds posted massive deficits as equity
portfolios collapsed. PREVIC (the pension supervisory authority) required deficit
funds to file formal recovery plans (planos de equacionamento), which in practice
mandated minimum government bond allocation floors and prohibited further risk-asset
accumulation. This converted what might have been a *voluntary* gradual reallocation
into a *mandatory* accelerated one. Without PREVIC enforcement, some funds likely
would have held impaired risk assets longer, waiting for recovery.

**4. FUNPRESP Structural Growth (~10%)**
A smaller but structural contribution. FUNPRESP, the complementary pension fund for
federal civil servants, was created in 2013 and reached meaningful AUM scale by
2015–2016. By design, it invests predominantly in federal securities. Its organic
growth added a steady, policy-driven bid for DPF that did not exist before 2013.
In BRL terms, FUNPRESP's contribution was modest (~R$10–15bn by 2016) relative to
the reallocation from legacy funds in the hundreds of billions.

### Summary

| Factor | Est. Contribution | Type |
|--------|:-----------------:|------|
| Equity/credit collapse (push out of risk) | ~40% | Market shock |
| Selic/NTN-B yield attraction (pull into DPF) | ~25% | Market shock |
| PREVIC de-risking mandates | ~25% | Regulatory |
| FUNPRESP structural growth | ~10% | Structural |

Factors 1 and 2 are two sides of the same macro shock (deep recession + Lava Jato).
Reframed structurally: **~65% market conditions forced the rotation**, **~25% regulation
locked it in**, **~10% new structural buyer**.

## Why Bank Holdings Increased in 2019

Banks (Instituições Financeiras) saw a step-up in DPF holdings in 2019, driven by
four converging forces.

### Four Contributing Factors

**1. Basel III LCR Reached 100% — January 2019 (~45%)**
Probably the single biggest factor. Brazil phased in the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)
requirement under CMN Resolution 4.401/2015 in annual steps: 70% (2015) → 80% (2017)
→ 90% (2018) → **100% in January 2019**. Banks are required to hold High Quality Liquid
Assets (HQLA) equal to 100% of projected 30-day stressed outflows — and HQLA in Brazil
is almost entirely federal securities (LFTs, LTNs, NTN-Bs). Hitting the final threshold
forced a simultaneous, system-wide step-up in mandatory DPF holdings. Unlike the pension
story (which was driven by market conditions), this was a hard regulatory deadline
affecting every systemically important bank at once.

**2. Selic Easing Cycle — Repositioning Ahead of Rate Cuts (~25%)**
The BCB held Selic at 6.5% from March 2018, then began cutting in July 2019 (6.5% →
4.5% by year-end). As rates fall, bond prices rise — banks that positioned in fixed-rate
(LTN/NTN-F) or IPCA-linked (NTN-B) bonds ahead of the cuts captured capital gains.
This created an incentive to build DPF inventory before the easing cycle began, and to
hold through it as prices appreciated.

**3. Pension Reform Approval — October 2019 (~20%)**
The approval of the pension reform (Reforma da Previdência) materially reduced Brazil's
long-term fiscal risk and compressed sovereign spreads. Banks accumulated government
bonds partly in anticipation of the spread compression trade: buy before the reform
passes, hold as prices appreciate on fiscal credibility gains. The reform's passage was
widely expected from mid-2019 after Guedes and Bolsonaro committed to it as a first-term
priority.

**4. Subdued Credit Demand — Excess Liquidity Parked in Bonds (~10%)**
Private sector credit growth was still sluggish in early 2019 as Brazil recovered slowly
from the 2015–2016 recession. Banks had excess reserves with limited high-quality loan
origination opportunities, so government securities absorbed the liquidity by default.

### Summary

| Factor | Est. Contribution | Type |
|--------|:-----------------:|------|
| LCR 100% phase-in (Jan 2019) | ~45% | Regulatory |
| Selic easing / bond repricing | ~25% | Market positioning |
| Pension reform spread compression | ~20% | Macro catalyst |
| Subdued credit demand | ~10% | Structural |

The LCR story dominates because it was a hard deadline affecting every systemically
important bank simultaneously — consistent with a discrete step-up in holdings rather
than a gradual trend.

## Why Investment Fund Holdings Topped Out Around 2019

Investment funds (Fundos de Investimento category) saw their DPF holdings plateau around
2019 after years of growth. Both the investor side (outflows from fixed income funds) and
the portfolio manager side (substitution into private credit) drove the stagnation.

### Four Contributing Factors

**1. Selic Cuts Made Fixed Income Funds Less Attractive (~35%)**
The easing cycle that began in July 2019 (6.5% → 4.5% by year-end, then 2.0% in 2020)
collapsed the return on DI/renda fixa funds — the bread-and-butter product that
predominantly holds LFTs. As fund yields approached zero in real terms, retail and
institutional investors began redeeming. Less AUM in fixed income mandates meant less
demand for DPF. The entire fixed income fund industry had been built around a structural
10–14% Selic; when that ended, the model broke down from the investor side.

**2. Great Rotation into Equities and FIIs (~30%)**
2019 was a record year for the Ibovespa and a boom year for Fundos de Investimento
Imobiliário (FIIs). Brazilian retail and institutional investors piled into equity funds,
FIIs, and multi-market funds chasing higher returns. Capital that previously sat in DI
funds recycled into these alternatives — and equity/FII funds do not hold DPF.

**3. CRI/CRA and Debenture Substitution Within Funds (~25%)**
Within fixed income mandates that remained, managers shifted allocations from government
bonds toward higher-yielding tax-exempt instruments: CRIs (real estate receivables), CRAs
(agribusiness receivables), and debentures incentivadas. These offer better after-tax
yields than LTNs/NTN-Bs for the same duration risk. The CRI/CRA market grew rapidly from
2017 onward precisely because the Selic was falling — issuers could raise competitively
and investors got a yield pickup vs. DPF. The structural consequence was fund managers
substituting out of DPF even within mandates that remained.

**4. Tesouro Direto Disintermediation (~10%)**
As Selic fell, paying 0.5–1.0% annual management fees on a DI fund earning 4–6% became
increasingly hard to justify. Retail investors shifted to buying LFTs and NTN-Bs directly
via Tesouro Direto, bypassing funds entirely. Tesouro Direto registered investors grew
from ~1.5M to 3M+ between 2018 and 2020.

### Summary

| Factor | Est. Contribution | Type |
|--------|:-----------------:|------|
| Fixed income fund outflows (Selic collapse) | ~35% | Macro/rates |
| Rotation to equities and FIIs | ~30% | Asset allocation shift |
| CRI/CRA/debenture substitution within funds | ~25% | Structural/product |
| Tesouro Direto disintermediation | ~10% | Structural |

The overarching story is the **end of Brazil's high-rate era**. The whole fixed income
fund industry had been built around a structural 10–14% Selic. When that ended, the
model broke down simultaneously from both sides: investors left for better returns
elsewhere, and managers substituted DPF for private credit instruments offering a
yield pickup.

## Why Foreign Holdings Topped Out in 2016

Foreign investor (Não-residentes) holdings of DPF peaked around 2015–2016 and stagnated
for several years thereafter. Four converging forces drove the exit.

### Four Contributing Factors

**1. BRL Depreciation Destroyed FX-Adjusted Returns (~35%)**
The BRL collapsed from ~R$2.20/USD in 2013 to ~R$4.00 by early 2016. For a USD-based
foreign investor holding NTN-Bs or LTNs unhedged, the currency loss wiped out the high
nominal yield entirely — a 14% Selic denominated in a currency that lost ~40% of its
value is deeply negative in USD terms. FX-hedged positions were prohibitively expensive
given the high interest rate differential. The risk/return proposition simply broke down.

**2. Sovereign Downgrade and Political Risk (~30%)**
2015–2016 was peak sovereign stress: Rousseff's impeachment process, Lava Jato in full
swing, Petrobras fraud, S&P downgraded Brazil to junk in September 2015, Moody's followed
in February 2016. Many institutional foreign mandates have hard floors prohibiting
sub-investment-grade holdings — the downgrade triggered forced selling regardless of
the investor's view on Brazil. Simultaneously, political risk was at its highest, adding
discretionary exits on top of mandate-driven ones.

**3. IOF Tax Barrier (~20%)**
Brazil had maintained a 6% IOF (Imposto sobre Operações Financeiras) tax on foreign
purchases of fixed income instruments since 2010, specifically designed to limit hot money
flows. Although reduced to 0% in June 2013, the structural barrier had already shaped
foreign participation — and with yields falling and BRL weakening, the after-tax return
profile became deeply unattractive even at 0% IOF. The tax history also signaled policy
risk: investors were wary it could be reimposed if BRL strengthened.

**4. Global EM Risk-Off / Fed Hiking Cycle (~15%)**
The Fed's first rate hike since 2006 came in December 2015, triggering a broad EM
risk-off and USD strengthening cycle. Brazil was among the most vulnerable EM
sovereigns given its twin deficits, rating deterioration, and political crisis coinciding
with the global tightening. Global investors systematically reduced EM fixed income
exposure, and Brazil — now sub-IG — was disproportionately affected.

### Summary

| Factor | Est. Contribution | Type |
|--------|:-----------------:|------|
| BRL depreciation (FX return destruction) | ~35% | Currency |
| Sovereign downgrade / political risk | ~30% | Credit |
| IOF tax barrier | ~20% | Regulatory |
| Global EM risk-off / Fed hiking | ~15% | External |

The BRL and sovereign downgrade stories are really one compound event: fiscal
deterioration → BRL weakness → rating cut → foreign exit. The external (Fed) factor
amplified but did not cause the dislocation — Brazil's problems were largely homegrown.

---

*Note: Contribution estimates are qualitative and based on relative order-of-magnitude
reasoning. Precise decomposition would require fund-level portfolio data (PREVIC/ABRAPP

funds; BCB balance of payments data for foreigners).*
