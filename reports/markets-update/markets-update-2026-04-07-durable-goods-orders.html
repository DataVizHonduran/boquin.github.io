1. Executive Summary

The latest Durable Goods data reveals a divergence between volatile headline orders and a steady, underlying trend in business investment. While headline New Orders experienced a modest month-on-month (MoM) contraction in February 2026, Core Capex (ex-aircraft) continues to exhibit a consistent upward trajectory, signaling that corporate confidence in long-term productivity investments remains intact despite a restrictive monetary backdrop.

Overall, the tone is one of "stable resilience." The lack of a systemic collapse in capital spending suggests that the economy is absorbing current interest rate levels without a significant freeze in business investment, reducing the urgency for aggressive monetary easing.

  • Headline Volatility: New Durable Goods Orders fell 1.3% MoM in February 2026 (from $319.9bn to $315.5bn), continuing a pattern of monthly swings that mask the underlying trend.
  • Core Capex Strength: Core Capex (ex-aircraft) rose 0.6% MoM in February 2026 to $79.4bn, marking a sustained growth trend from the $75.6bn level seen in June 2025.

  • Decoupling of Orders: The gap between headline orders and Core Capex suggests that volatility is being driven by non-core components (likely transport/aircraft), while the "industrial heart" of the economy remains steady.
  • Investment Floor: The consistent climb in Core Capex through Q4 2025 and into Q1 2026 suggests a high floor for economic activity, limiting the probability of a hard landing.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: Growth appears stable but moderate. The steady climb in Core Capex provides a reliable contribution to GDP via private domestic investment, offsetting the erratic nature of headline durable orders.

(ii) Labor Market: While this data is a leading indicator rather than a direct measure, the sustained growth in capital expenditures typically correlates with a demand for skilled labor to install and maintain new equipment, suggesting continued support for employment in the industrial and tech sectors.

(iii) Inflation: The persistent demand for durable capital goods suggests that pricing power in the B2B sector remains firm. This indicates that "sticky" inflation may persist in the goods sector, as firms continue to invest despite higher borrowing costs.

4. Fiscal Commentary

There is no direct fiscal data provided; however, the steady rise in Core Capex may reflect the delayed transmission of fiscal incentives (e.g., tax credits for green energy or semiconductor manufacturing). If this investment is subsidized by government credits, it reduces the sensitivity of corporate spending to Fed rate hikes, thereby weakening the transmission mechanism of monetary policy.

5. Policy Outlook

Next Move: Hold / Neutral

The data supports a "higher for longer" or "steady" stance. With Core Capex growing 5.1% YoY and showing no signs of contraction, there is no evidence of a "credit crunch" or investment collapse that would necessitate an emergency rate cut. Given the resilience of business spending, the Fed is likely to maintain current rates in the next meeting, awaiting further confirmation of inflation cooling before pivoting to a loosening cycle. The balance of risks is tilted toward inflation persistence rather than growth collapse.
