3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: Growth is currently in an acceleration phase. The trajectory of durable goods orders—rising from $314.1bn in Sept 2025 to $345.4bn in Sept 2026—indicates a robust expansion in industrial activity and a strong appetite for capital expenditure.

(ii) Labor Market: While this is a goods-sector print, the 100th percentile reading in Core Capex implies high utilization of industrial capacity. This typically translates to tight labor markets in manufacturing and construction, likely sustaining wage pressure in these sectors.

(iii) Inflation: The data is inflationary. When investment reaches the 100th percentile of a decade-long range, bottlenecks typically emerge. The aggressive demand for capital goods suggests that input prices for machinery and equipment are likely rising, feeding into core goods inflation.

4. Cyclical Alignment

The current regime is classified as Late-Cycle Overheating.

With a Z-score of +2.12σ for total orders and a significant +2.52σ for Core Capex, the economy has moved beyond a "mid-cycle pause" and is now operating in a statistical outlier zone. Any Z-score exceeding |2.0| is a regime-defining event; in this case, the alignment of both metrics at the extreme top of their 10-year ranges suggests the cycle is peaking and the economy is operating above its sustainable potential.

5. Policy Outlook

Forecast: Hawkish Hold / Potential Hike

The Fed is facing a classic "overheating" scenario. With Core Capex at a 10-year high, the risk of a structural inflation spike outweighs the risk of a growth slowdown. Given that investment is at the 100th percentile, the Fed cannot justify easing.

We expect the Fed to maintain a restrictive stance (Hold) in the immediate term to cool this investment frenzy. However, if the next print shows Core Capex sustaining these +2.5σ levels, we anticipate a preemptive 25bps hike to prevent the late-cycle peak from evolving into a hard-landing scenario. The balance of risks has shifted decisively toward inflation.
