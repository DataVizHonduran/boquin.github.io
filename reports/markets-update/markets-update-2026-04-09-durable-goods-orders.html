1. Executive Summary

The February 2026 Durable Goods report presents a bifurcated narrative. While headline new orders experienced a modest monthly contraction, the underlying Core Capital Expenditure (Capex) component—excluding volatile aircraft orders—remained resilient. This divergence suggests that while broad-based demand is subject to cyclical swings, business investment in productivity-enhancing equipment remains on a steady growth trajectory.

Overall, the data signals a "stable-growth" environment. The persistence of core investment suggests that firms are continuing to expand capacity and modernize, mitigating concerns of a sharp industrial slowdown. From a policy perspective, this supports a narrative of economic resilience, reducing the urgency for aggressive monetary easing while providing no evidence of overheating.

2. Five Main Views

  • Headline Contraction: Total Durable Goods New Orders fell by 1.37% month-on-month (MoM) in February 2026, dropping to \$315.5bn from \$319.9bn in January.
  • Core Capex Strength: Core Capex Orders (ex-aircraft) rose 0.58% MoM to \$79.4bn, continuing a consistent upward trend observed throughout 2025.
  • Robust Annual Growth: Despite the monthly dip, headline orders remain significantly higher on a year-on-year (YoY) basis, up 7.32% compared to February 2025 (\$294.0bn).
  • Investment Persistence: Core Capex has grown 5.11% YoY, reflecting a disciplined and steady increase in business investment rather than a volatile spike.
  • Sectoral Divergence: The gap between the headline decline (-1.37%) and core growth (+0.58%) indicates that the monthly weakness is concentrated in non-core sectors, likely transport or defense, rather than general business investment.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: The data points to a stable, if unspectacular, growth profile. The steady climb of Core Capex from \$75.6bn in February 2025 to \$79.4bn in February 2026 suggests a healthy investment cycle. While headline volatility persists, the underlying trend in capital spending acts as a floor for GDP growth, indicating that the private sector is still betting on future capacity expansion.

(ii) Labor Market: The resilience in Core Capex is a positive signal for industrial employment. Because capital goods production is labor-intensive and requires skilled technical oversight, the consistent MoM growth in this segment suggests stable demand for manufacturing labor and a low probability of immediate, widespread layoffs in the industrial sector.

(iii) Inflation: From a pricing perspective, the steady nature of core investment—rather than an exponential surge—suggests that demand for capital goods is not currently driving significant "cost-push" inflation. However, the 5.11% YoY increase in core orders ensures that demand remains tight, which may keep nominal prices for industrial equipment elevated.

5. Policy Outlook

Based on the current data, we forecast the Federal Reserve will maintain a "Hold" posture at the next FOMC meeting.

The balance of risks is currently skewed toward a "soft landing." The 1.37% MoM dip in headline orders provides the Fed with the necessary cover to avoid further tightening, as it suggests the economy is not overheating. Conversely, the steady 0.58% MoM growth in Core Capex removes the imperative for emergency rate cuts, as business investment remains robust. We expect the Fed to remain data-dependent, waiting for a more definitive signal from inflation prints before committing to a downward trajectory, as the real economy—evidenced by core investment—remains fundamentally sound.
