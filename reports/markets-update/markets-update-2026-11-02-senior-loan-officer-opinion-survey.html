1. Executive Summary

The latest Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (SLOOS) data indicates a significant decompression of credit conditions across both corporate and consumer segments. The net percentage of banks tightening standards for large firm C&I loans and credit cards has retreated from the restrictive peaks of 2023, settling near the 10-year median.

The overall tone is one of "normalization." The aggressive tightening cycle that characterized the early 2020s has fully dissipated, replaced by a neutral credit stance. This suggests that financial conditions are no longer acting as a primary drag on economic activity, shifting the burden of growth support back to fundamental demand and monetary policy.

2. Five Main Views

  • C&I Normalization: Net tightening for large firm C&I loans has collapsed from a high of 33.9% (Oct '23) to 1.7% (Oct '26), signaling a near-total removal of the restrictive credit overlay.
  • Consumer Credit Easing: Credit card standards have followed a similar trajectory, falling from 28.9% to 4.0%, indicating that lenders have regained confidence in household balance sheets.
  • Volatility in Corporate Lending: The 2025 spike in C&I tightening (reaching 18.5% in April '25) suggests a period of transient risk aversion that has since been resolved.
  • Mean Reversion: With Z-scores of -0.24$\sigma$ for C&I and -0.18$\sigma$ for credit cards, credit conditions are currently orbiting their 10-year averages, removing them as a source of systemic tail risk.
  • Credit Availability: The move toward 0.0% net tightening suggests a transition from "restrictive" to "neutral," effectively widening the window for corporate refinancing and consumer spending.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: The data suggests a supportive environment for GDP. The transition from high tightening (33.9%) to near-zero (1.7%) in C&I loans removes a significant headwind for capital expenditure (CapEx) and business expansion.

(ii) Labor Market: While SLOOS is a proxy for credit, the easing of credit card standards (down to 4.0%) implies a stabilization in consumer solvency, which typically correlates with a resilient labor market and steady nominal wage growth.

(iii) Inflation: The shift toward neutral credit conditions is potentially inflationary. By easing the "credit squeeze," the financial system is increasing the velocity of money and supporting aggregate demand, which may complicate the final mile of inflation targeting.

4. Cyclical Alignment

Based on the 10-year Z-scores (-0.24$\sigma$ and -0.18$\sigma$) and percentiles (52.5th and 49.2nd), the current regime is classified as a mid-cycle pause. We are seeing neither the extreme tightening associated with late-cycle overheating (which would see Z-scores > +2.0) nor the systemic collapse associated with a crisis. The data reflects a structural return to the long-term mean, suggesting the economy has moved past the volatility of the post-pandemic tightening cycle into a period of stability.

5. Policy Outlook

Forecast: Hold / Neutral Bias

The data suggests the Fed has successfully navigated the "tightening" phase without triggering a credit crunch. Because credit conditions have normalized organically (moving from the 80th+ percentile down to the 50th), the Fed no longer needs to use aggressive rate cuts to "unfreeze" the credit markets.

Given that credit is now neutral and no longer restrictive, the balance of risks has shifted toward inflation. We expect the Fed to maintain current rates in the near term to ensure inflation remains anchored, as the financial system is now providing sufficient liquidity to support growth without further monetary stimulus. Any future move is likely to be a slow, data-dependent glide path toward a neutral rate.
