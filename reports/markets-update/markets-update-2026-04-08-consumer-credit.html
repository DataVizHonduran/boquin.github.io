Flash Note: Consumer Credit (G.19) Analysis

1. Executive Summary

The latest G.19 data reveals a resilient, albeit steady, expansion in US consumer leverage. Total consumer credit rose to $5.12 trillion in February 2026, maintaining a consistent upward trajectory throughout the period. The data suggests that household balance sheets remain robust enough to absorb current borrowing costs, with credit growth driven primarily by non-revolving loans.

The overall tone is one of "persistent consumption support." The lack of a significant contraction in revolving credit, despite a restrictive rate environment, indicates that consumer spending is being sustained by a combination of credit expansion and likely wealth effects, reducing the immediate pressure on the Fed to pivot toward aggressive easing to stimulate demand.

2. Five Main Views

  • Steady Aggregate Growth: Total consumer credit grew by 0.18% MoM in February 2026 and expanded by 2.2% YoY (from Feb 2025 to Feb 2026), signaling a stable appetite for leverage.
  • Non-Revolving Dominance: The primary engine of growth is non-revolving credit (student loans, auto loans), which rose from $3.65 trillion in Feb 2025 to $3.79 trillion in Feb 2026 (+3.7%), outpacing revolving credit growth.
  • Revolving Credit Resilience: Revolving credit (credit cards) showed a modest MoM increase of 0.05% in February 2026, ending the period at $1.33 trillion, up 1.7% YoY.
  • Consistent Momentum: Total credit has increased for 8 consecutive months (July 2025 – Feb 2026), suggesting a structural trend of increasing household debt rather than a cyclical spike.
  • Low Volatility: The narrow range of MoM changes suggests a controlled expansion, with no signs of a "credit crunch" or a sudden surge in distressed borrowing.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: The data points to a supportive environment for GDP. The steady rise in total credit—specifically the $135 billion increase in non-revolving credit over the last year—suggests continued investment in durable goods and education, which underpins long-term consumption growth.

(ii) Labor Market: While G.19 is a credit metric, the ability of consumers to increase their total debt load to $5.12 trillion implies a baseline of employment stability. The lack of a contraction in revolving credit suggests that households are not currently forced to aggressively deleverage, which typically occurs during periods of rising unemployment.

(iii) Inflation: The persistence of credit growth acts as a tailwind for aggregate demand. With total credit expanding by 2.2% YoY, the "wealth effect" and availability of credit may keep core inflation sticky, as consumers continue to spend despite higher nominal borrowing costs.

4. Fiscal Commentary

There is no direct fiscal data in this release; however, the steady rise in non-revolving credit may reflect the lagging impact of federal student loan policies or subsidized credit markets. From a monetary transmission perspective, the continued growth in household leverage suggests that the Fed's policy rate hikes are not yet causing a sharp contraction in credit demand, potentially slowing the transmission of tight monetary policy to the real economy.

5. Policy Outlook

Next Move: Hold / Neutral

The data provides no catalyst for an emergency rate cut, as consumer credit demand remains healthy and households are not showing signs of financial distress. Conversely, the steady 2.2% YoY growth in total credit suggests that demand is not cooling rapidly enough to guarantee a swift return to the 2% inflation target. We expect the Fed to maintain its current stance in the next meeting, as the balance of risks remains tilted toward inflation persistence rather than a credit-driven recession. We forecast a "higher for longer" plateau until a more pronounced deceleration in credit growth or a softening in the labor market emerges.
