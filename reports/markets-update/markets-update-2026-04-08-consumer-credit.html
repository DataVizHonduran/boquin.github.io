Flash Note: Consumer Credit Trends Signal Persistent Demand Amid Tightening Financial Conditions

1. Executive Summary

The latest G.19 data reveals a resilient, albeit slow, expansion in total consumer credit, which reached $5.12 trillion in February 2026. The trajectory is characterized by a steady climb in non-revolving credit (auto loans, student loans) and a more volatile but upward-trending revolving credit component.

The overall tone is one of cautious consumption; households continue to leverage their balance sheets to maintain spending levels. For the Federal Reserve, this suggests that monetary policy is not yet inducing a sharp contraction in consumer borrowing, potentially complicating the path toward a neutral rate if credit-fueled spending continues to support aggregate demand.

2. Five Main Views

  • Steady Aggregate Growth: Total consumer credit grew by 0.18% MoM in February 2026 and has expanded by 2.2% since February 2025, indicating a persistent appetite for leverage.
  • Non-Revolving Dominance: Non-revolving credit remains the primary driver of growth, increasing from $3.65 trillion (Feb '25) to $3.79 trillion (Feb '26), a year-over-year increase of 3.7%.
  • Revolving Credit Stabilization: After a period of volatility in early 2025, revolving credit has trended upward since June 2025, rising 1.7% over the last 12 months, suggesting a gradual return to credit card reliance.
  • Consistent Monthly Momentum: Total credit has seen positive MoM growth in 11 of the last 13 months, reflecting a highly stable trend of credit accumulation.
  • Low Sensitivity to Rates: The continued rise in both revolving and non-revolving credit suggests that current borrowing costs have not yet reached a "breaking point" for the average US consumer.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: The data points to a supportive environment for GDP. The consistent MoM increase in total credit—specifically the $8.8bn jump between January and February 2026—suggests that consumer spending is being underpinned by credit expansion, mitigating the risk of a near-term consumption cliff.

(ii) Labor Market: While G.19 is a credit metric, the steady increase in non-revolving credit (often tied to auto loans) implies a baseline level of consumer confidence in future income streams. The lack of a contraction in revolving credit suggests that households are not yet facing a systemic liquidity crisis or widespread unemployment.

(iii) Inflation: The trend is potentially inflationary. As consumers continue to draw on credit to fund purchases, aggregate demand remains elevated. The 1.7% YoY increase in revolving credit indicates that "lifestyle" spending is not being curtailed, which may keep service-sector inflation sticky.

4. Policy Outlook

Based on the resilience of consumer borrowing, we expect the Federal Reserve to maintain a hawkish hold in the immediate term. The data shows no evidence of the "credit crunch" necessary to rapidly cool the economy. Given that total credit is at a cyclical high ($5.12tn) and growing, the balance of risks tilts toward inflation persistence rather than growth collapse. We forecast the Fed will hold rates steady for the next two meetings, with any pivot to cuts contingent on a visible deceleration in credit growth or a spike in delinquency rates (not captured here), as the current borrowing behavior provides the Fed ample room to keep policy restrictive.
