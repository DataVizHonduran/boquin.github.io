Sector Note: US Construction Spending Analysis

1. Executive Summary

The latest construction spending data reveals a divergence between residential volatility and nonresidential resilience. While total spending remains elevated relative to the 10-year average, the most recent print shows a contraction in aggregate activity, driven primarily by a sharp pullback in private residential investment.

The overarching signal is one of "asymmetric cooling." The nonresidential sector continues to push toward decade-highs, likely supported by structural tailwinds (industrial policy/data centers), while the residential sector exhibits high sensitivity to current financing conditions. This suggests that while the broader economy is not in a freefall, the interest-rate-sensitive components of GDP are under significant pressure.

2. Five Main Views

  • Residential Retrenchment: Private residential spending plummeted by 9.9% MoM in September 2026 ($75.6bn vs $83.9bn in August), erasing gains made over the previous four months.
  • Nonresidential Strength: Private nonresidential spending continues its bullish trajectory, rising 0.6% MoM to $1.32tn, maintaining a dominant 97.5th percentile position over the 10-year regime.
  • Aggregate Contraction: Total construction spending fell 1.8% MoM in September, reflecting the inability of nonresidential growth to offset the residential slump.
  • Structural Divergence: The Z-score gap between nonresidential (+1.47σ) and residential (+0.82σ) highlights a decoupling of commercial/industrial investment from the housing market.
  • Year-on-Year Erosion: Total spending in September 2026 ($2.186tn) is down approximately 2.7% compared to September 2025 ($2.246tn), indicating a slow-bleed in aggregate capacity expansion.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: Aggregate growth is decelerating. The trend from late 2025 through 2026 shows a series of peaks and troughs, but the current trajectory is negative. The reliance on nonresidential spending to carry the growth narrative is increasing, which limits the overall upside to GDP.

(ii) Labor Market: The data suggests a bifurcated labor outlook. The sustained strength in nonresidential construction (near the 10Y ceiling) likely supports high-skilled industrial and commercial trade employment. However, the volatility in residential spending threatens blue-collar residential construction jobs.

(iii) Inflation: The persistent 97.5th percentile level of nonresidential spending suggests that demand for industrial inputs and commercial materials remains inelastic, which may provide a floor for core goods inflation despite broader cooling.

4. Cyclical Alignment

Based on the provided metrics, the current regime is classified as a 'mid-cycle' pause with emerging fragility.

With a total spending Z-score of +1.14σ and a 75.8th percentile ranking, the economy is not yet in a state of 'late-cycle' overheating (which would typically require Z-scores > |2.0|). However, the sharp residential drop-off suggests the "pause" is becoming lopsided. We are not seeing a structural regime shift, as the nonresidential sector remains anchored to its long-term growth trend, but the residential sector is behaving as a leading indicator of tightening financial conditions.

5. Policy Outlook

Next Fed Move: Hold / Dovish Pivot (Timing: Next Meeting)

The data supports a pause or a cautious move toward easing. The significant MoM collapse in residential spending ($8.3bn decline in one month) signals that the "higher for longer" regime is now actively suppressing housing investment. While the nonresidential sector is not yet overheating (Z-score +1.47σ is strong but below the +2.0σ threshold for systemic overheating), the risk of a residential hard landing now outweighs the risk of nonresidential inflation. We expect the Fed to maintain current rates but shift its forward guidance toward a cut in the next cycle to stabilize the housing market.
