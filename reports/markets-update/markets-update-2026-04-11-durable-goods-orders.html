Subject: Durable Goods Analysis: Core Capex Resilience Offsets Headline Volatility

1. Executive Summary

The latest Durable Goods data presents a bifurcated picture of the US industrial landscape. While headline New Orders exhibited a modest monthly contraction in February, the underlying Core Capital Expenditure (Capex) trend remains remarkably robust. This divergence suggests that while broad-based demand for durable goods is subject to cyclical volatility, strategic business investment in non-defense capital equipment is continuing its steady expansion.

From a policy perspective, the persistence of core investment provides a critical buffer against growth concerns. The data reinforces a "soft landing" narrative, suggesting that business confidence remains high despite the restrictive interest rate environment. We view this as a signal that the economy is not in a state of fragility, reducing the urgency for the Federal Reserve to accelerate easing.

2. Five Main Views

  • Headline Softness: New Durable Goods Orders fell by 1.3% month-on-month (MoM) in February 2026, dropping to $315.9bn from $320.0bn in January.
  • Strong Annual Momentum: Despite the monthly dip, headline orders remain significantly higher on a year-over-year (YoY) basis, growing 7.4% compared to February 2025 ($294.0bn).
  • Core Capex Resilience: Core Capex (ex-aircraft) continues its disciplined ascent, rising 0.7% MoM to $79.5bn, marking a consistent upward trajectory since early 2025.
  • Investment Quality: The YoY growth in Core Capex (+5.2%) is less volatile than headline orders, indicating a structural commitment to capacity expansion and productivity enhancements.
  • Divergence Trend: The widening gap between the volatility of headline orders and the stability of Core Capex suggests that "noise" (likely from transport or volatile sectors) is masking a very healthy business investment cycle.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: The data points to a resilient growth trajectory underpinned by business investment. The steady climb of Core Capex from $75.6bn in February 2025 to $79.5bn in February 2026 indicates that firms are continuing to invest in long-term assets, which typically serves as a leading indicator for GDP expansion.

(ii) Labor Market: While this data is a proxy, the sustained growth in capital equipment orders suggests a focus on capital deepening. This typically supports labor productivity gains and implies a stable demand for high-skilled technical labor to implement and maintain new capital assets, mitigating risks of a sharp spike in unemployment.

(iii) Inflation: The consistent demand for core durable goods suggests that pricing power remains intact within the manufacturing sector. While not a direct measure of CPI, the lack of a collapse in orders suggests that businesses are not yet seeing a dramatic cooling in demand that would force aggressive price cuts, potentially keeping a floor under goods-sector inflation.

5. Policy Outlook

We forecast the Federal Reserve will maintain a "Hold" or "Slow-Cut" posture at the next FOMC meeting. The balance of risks has shifted away from a growth collapse toward a managed normalization. With Core Capex growing steadily and headline orders still 7.4% above last year's levels, there is no data-driven justification for an aggressive easing cycle. We expect the Fed to remain data-dependent, likely delaying further rate cuts until there is evidence that the resilience in business investment is waning or inflation reaches the 2% target more decisively.
