1. Executive Summary

The latest G.19 release indicates a persistent and accelerating expansion in US consumer credit, with Total Consumer Credit reaching a 10-year peak of $5.19 trillion. The data reveals a synchronized rise across both revolving and nonrevolving components, though the acceleration in revolving credit is particularly noteworthy, suggesting a heightened reliance on high-cost borrowing to sustain consumption levels.

Overall, the tone is one of late-cycle resilience bordering on overheating. With all three tracked series sitting in the 100th percentile of their 10-year ranges, the US consumer is operating at a historical maximum of leverage. This provides a strong tailwind for nominal GDP growth but creates significant headwinds for the Federal Reserve’s inflation mandate, as credit-fueled demand remains robust despite restrictive nominal rates.

2. Five Main Views

  • Historical Peak Leverage: Total Consumer Credit has reached the 100th percentile of its 10-year range ($5.186 trillion), with a Z-score of +1.44$\sigma$, signaling an extreme accumulation of household debt.
  • Revolving Credit Acceleration: Revolving credit is the primary driver of cyclical risk, posting the highest Z-score (+1.61$\sigma$) and a year-on-year (YoY) increase of 3.58% (from $1.310tn in July 2025 to $1.357tn in July 2026).
  • Steady Nonrevolving Growth: Nonrevolving credit grew by 2.23% YoY, reaching $3.829 trillion. While slower than revolving credit, the consistent month-on-month (MoM) climb indicates continued demand for installment loans and mortgages.
  • Momentum Shift: The MoM growth in Total Credit for July 2026 was approximately 0.35%, maintaining a steady upward trajectory that has not decelerated since Q1 2026.
  • Credit Quality Divergence: The fact that credit continues to expand into the 100th percentile suggests that lenders have not yet significantly tightened standards, or that consumer income growth is still perceived as sufficient to service this record debt load.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: The data describes a growth regime characterized by strong aggregate demand. The continuous expansion of total credit—rising from $5.056 trillion to $5.186 trillion over the last 12 months—suggests that consumption is being aggressively supported by borrowing, which likely prevents a significant slowdown in real GDP.

(ii) Labor Market: While G.19 is a credit series, the 100th percentile credit expansion implies a tight labor market. Lenders typically only extend credit to these historical levels when employment is broad-based and wage growth is perceived as stable enough to support debt service. There is no evidence of a "credit crunch" that would typically accompany a softening labor market.

(iii) Inflation: The credit data points to persistent inflationary pressure. The 3.58% YoY increase in revolving credit suggests that consumers are utilizing credit cards and lines of credit to maintain their standard of living, which sustains demand-pull inflation and complicates the path toward a 2% target.
