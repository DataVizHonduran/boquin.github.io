Subject: JOLTS Analysis – Labor Market Normalization and the Return of Worker Confidence

1. Executive Summary

The September JOLTS print suggests a labor market that has successfully transitioned from the post-pandemic volatility toward a stable, sustainable equilibrium. The data reveals a synchronized uptick in job openings, hires, and quits, signaling a broad-based recovery in business confidence and worker mobility without a corresponding spike in layoffs.

From a policy perspective, the print is "Goldilocks" in nature. The absence of distress in the Layoffs & Discharges rate, paired with a moderate increase in the Quits rate, suggests that the labor market is neither overheating nor cratering. This provides the Federal Reserve with significant flexibility to prioritize a neutral policy stance, as the risk of a systemic labor market shock has diminished.

2. Five Main Views

  • Resilient Demand: Job openings rose to 7.52M in September, a +4.2% MoM increase from August, indicating that firms are continuing to expand or maintain headcount.
  • Hiring Momentum: Hires climbed to 5.40M, the highest level in the provided 13-month window, suggesting a tightening of the gap between vacancies and fills.
  • Worker Confidence Rebound: The Quits rate hit 2.1% in September, a significant jump from 1.9% in August, signaling that workers are once again feeling confident enough to switch roles for better pay or conditions.
  • Structural Stability: The Layoffs & Discharges rate remains anchored at 1.0%, sitting in the 30.8th percentile of the 10Y range, confirming that the current labor tightening is not being offset by involuntary separations.
  • Normalization of the "Great Resignation": With a Z-score of -0.18$\sigma$ for Quits, the market has fully exited the extreme volatility of the early 2020s and is now operating within a standard historical distribution.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: The simultaneous rise in job openings and hires suggests a positive growth trajectory. Businesses are not merely hoarding labor but are actively recruiting, which typically precedes an expansion in capital expenditure and output.

(ii) Labor Market: The market is characterized by high stability and moderate tightness. The "churn" is returning—evidenced by the rise in quits—but the floor is solid, as layoffs remain near historical lows. The labor market is functioning as a healthy mechanism for resource reallocation.

(iii) Inflation: While the rise in the Quits rate to 2.1% could theoretically signal renewed wage pressure, the overall Z-scores for openings (-0.21$\sigma$) and quits (-0.18$\sigma$) suggest we are far from the "overheating" levels that drove the 2021-2022 inflation spike. Wage-push inflation risks remain contained.

4. Cyclical Alignment

Based on the 10-year Z-scores, the current regime is a mid-cycle pause. None of the primary indicators exceed the $|2.0|\sigma$ threshold required to signal a regime-defining event or a structural shift. Job openings (63.3rd percentile) and Quits (47.5th percentile) are hovering near their long-term means. We are seeing a stabilization phase where the economy has absorbed previous shocks and is operating within a standard historical corridor.

5. Policy Outlook

Next Move: Hold / Neutral Bias

The data supports a "hold" at the next FOMC meeting. The balance of risks has shifted: the risk of a labor market collapse is negated by the 1.0% layoff rate, while the risk of a wage-price spiral is mitigated by the fact that job openings are still below the 10Y median (Z-score -0.21$\sigma$).

Given the stability of the JOLTS data, the Fed is likely to maintain current rates to ensure inflation reaches target without needing to trigger a recession. We forecast no immediate direction change unless the Quits rate continues to accelerate toward the 2.5%+ range, which would signal a return to late-cycle overheating.
