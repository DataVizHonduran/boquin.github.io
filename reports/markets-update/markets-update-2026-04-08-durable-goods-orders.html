1. Executive Summary

The latest Durable Goods data reveals a divergence between volatile headline orders and a steady, underlying trend in business investment. While headline New Orders experienced a slight contraction in February 2026, the "Core Capex" component (excluding aircraft) continues to exhibit a consistent upward trajectory, signaling that corporate confidence in long-term productivity investments remains intact despite broader macroeconomic headwinds.

The overall tone is one of "stable resilience." The lack of a sharp collapse in core capital spending suggests that the restrictive policy environment has not yet triggered a systemic retreat in business investment, limiting the urgency for the Federal Reserve to pivot aggressively toward easing.

2. Five Main Views

  • Headline Volatility: New Durable Goods orders fell 1.3% MoM in February 2026 ($315.5bn vs $319.9bn), continuing a pattern of month-to-month instability seen throughout 2025.
  • Core Capex Strength: Core Capex (ex-aircraft) rose 0.5% MoM in February 2026 to $79.4bn, marking a sustained growth trend from the $75.6bn level seen in June 2025.
  • Year-on-Year Expansion: Core Capex has grown 5.1% YoY (Feb '26 vs Feb '25), indicating a robust expansion in non-aircraft business equipment and structures.
  • Decoupling of Demand: The gap between headline orders and core capex suggests that volatility is being driven by cyclical consumer durables or volatile transport sectors rather than a decay in industrial investment.
  • Investment Floor: The consistent climb in core orders from September 2025 through February 2026 suggests a high "floor" for business spending, likely supported by digitalization and automation trends.

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: Real GDP growth is receiving a steady tailwind from private domestic investment. The steady climb in Core Capex suggests that the investment component of GDP is resilient, offsetting the choppy demand seen in headline durable goods.

(ii) Labor Market: The sustained demand for capital goods typically correlates with a need for skilled labor to install and maintain new equipment. This suggests a continued structural demand for technical labor, which may keep nominal wages sticky even as the broader economy cools.

(iii) Inflation: The persistent growth in capital orders suggests that firms are still investing to expand capacity or efficiency. While this is long-term disinflationary (via productivity gains), the immediate demand for these goods prevents a rapid collapse in producer prices (PPI) for durable goods.

4. Fiscal Commentary

There is no direct fiscal data in this release; however, the steady rise in Core Capex may reflect the lagging effects of industrial policy (e.g., CHIPS Act or Inflation Reduction Act incentives). If these investments are fiscally subsidized, it complicates monetary transmission by insulating corporate investment from higher interest rates, potentially requiring the Fed to keep rates "higher for longer" to achieve the same restrictive effect.

5. Policy Outlook

Next Move: Hold / Neutral

Timing: Next FOMC Meeting

The data supports a "Hold" stance. The 5.1% YoY growth in Core Capex indicates that the economy is not in a state of contraction and that business investment is not breaking under current rates. With the investment engine humming and no signs of a "capex cliff," the Fed has ample room to maintain current levels to ensure inflation is fully extinguished. We see the balance of risks tilted toward a "higher for longer" scenario, as the resilience in core business spending reduces the risk of a hard landing.
