1. Executive Summary

The latest Durable Goods data reveals a divergence between volatile headline orders and a steady, upward trajectory in core capital expenditures (Capex). While headline new orders showed a modest contraction in February 2026, the underlying investment appetite remains robust, with Core Capex (ex-aircraft) continuing its consistent climb.

The overall tone is one of "stable resilience." The lack of a significant drawdown in business investment suggests that the private sector is absorbing current borrowing costs without a systemic retreat in capacity expansion. For the Federal Reserve, this provides a cushion against growth concerns but suggests that demand-side pressures in the investment sector remain sticky.

2. Five Main Views

  • Headline Softness is Noise: New Durable Goods orders fell 1.3% MoM in February 2026 ($315.5bn vs $319.9bn), but this remains significantly elevated compared to the early 2025 baseline ($294bn).
  • Core Capex Momentum: Core Capex (ex-aircraft) grew 0.5% MoM in February to $79.4bn, marking a consistent trend of growth since June 2025.
  • Strong Annual Investment Growth: Core Capex has risen 5.1% YoY (Feb '26 vs Feb '25), signaling a sustained commitment to productivity-enhancing investments.
  • Volatility in Non-Core Components: The gap between the headline decline (-1.3%) and the core increase (+0.5%) indicates that the February dip was driven by volatile sectors (likely transportation/aircraft) rather than a broad business cycle downturn.
  • Investment Floor Established: The steady climb in `NEWORDER` data from $75.6bn in June 2025 to $79.4bn in February 2026 suggests a high floor for business spending, reducing the likelihood of a sudden "capex cliff."

3. Macro Characterization

(i) Growth: The data supports a "slow but steady" growth narrative. While headline orders are choppy, the consistent growth in Core Capex suggests that the primary engine of long-term GDP—business investment—is functioning efficiently and expanding.

(ii) Labor Market: While this is a goods-order series, the sustained demand for durable capital equipment typically correlates with stable employment in manufacturing and installation services. The lack of a Capex contraction suggests no immediate catalyst for industrial layoffs.

(iii) Inflation: The persistent demand for capital goods, evidenced by the 5.1% YoY increase in Core Capex, suggests that pricing power in the B2B sector remains intact. This indicates that investment-driven demand is not yet cooling sufficiently to provide a strong disinflationary impulse.

5. Policy Outlook

Forecast: Hold / Neutral

The balance of risks currently favors a "Hold" for the next FOMC meeting. The resilience in Core Capex ($79.4bn) suggests that the economy is not in a state of distress that would warrant an emergency rate cut. Conversely, the slight MoM dip in headline orders is not severe enough to signal a looming recession.

Given that business investment is continuing to expand YoY, the Fed is likely to maintain its current restrictive or neutral stance to ensure inflation reaches its target, as there is no evidence of a "credit crunch" or a collapse in business confidence. We expect the Fed to remain data-dependent, awaiting a more definitive softening in core investment before pivoting toward easing.
